Start Here

A sampling distribution is not the data in one sample. It is the probability distribution of a statistic over every possible random sample of a fixed size from the same population.

Population distribution

Values of individual X. Axis: possible observation values.

One sample

A set of observations actually drawn, such as 25 marks.

One statistic

A single number calculated from that sample: X, p, or a difference.

Sampling distribution

All possible values of that statistic and their probabilities.

The first exam question

What is random? One observation X, an average X, a proportion p, or a difference X1−X2? The answer determines the model.

Core Vocabulary

Population

The complete collection of units of interest.

Sample

A subset selected to learn about the population.

Parameter

A fixed, usually unknown population number: μ, σ, π.

Statistic

A sample-based random quantity: X or p.

Sample-to-sample variation

Different random samples contain different units, so their statistics differ.

Standard error

The standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution.

Knowledge check: parameter or statistic?

A university's true proportion of commuters is π, a parameter. The proportion in a random sample of 200 students is p, a statistic.

One realised sample mean is a point. The sampling distribution is the whole landscape of points that could have occurred before sampling. Probability statements about X refer to that landscape.

We rarely take every possible sample. The distribution is a mathematical model of what repeated random sampling would produce.

Standard Error and Unbiasedness

Unbiasedness describes the centre of repeated estimates; standard error describes their sample-to-sample spread. A statistic may be unbiased and still miss μ in one particular sample.

Formal definition

E(X)=μ, so the long-run mean of all possible sample means equals the population mean.

Intuition

Random samples sometimes overestimate and sometimes underestimate. With a valid random design, those deviations balance in the long run.

Recognition clue

Words such as “how much sample means vary” ask for the standard error, not the population standard deviation.

Common mistake

Unbiased does not mean “every sample is correct”; it means no systematic long-run over- or under-estimation.

Centre and spread

μX = μ

σX = σn

Symbols: σ is population SD; n is sample size; σX is the SD of all possible sample means.

Conditions: independent observations from a random sample; with-replacement sampling or a negligible sampling fraction. Otherwise apply the FPC.

The 1/√n law

Quadruple n to halve the SE. Increasing n from 25 to 100 does not quarter it. The centre remains μ.

Compare the denominator √n for the two sample sizes before doing any probability calculation.
Knowledge check: σ=12. Compare n=9 and n=36.

Method: keep σ fixed and change only n. SE₉=12/√9=4; SE₃₆=12/√36=2. Four times the sample size halves the SE.

8

Normal Population Case

For a random sample with independent observations drawn from a normally distributed population, the sampling distribution of the mean is normal for every valid sample size. Population normality alone is not a substitute for a valid sampling design.

Exact model

X∼N(μ,σ²) and a random independent sample imply X∼N(μ,σ²/n).

This is exact, not a CLT approximation. Its centre is μ and its standard error is σ/√n.

Why it works

A linear combination of independent normal observations is itself normal; an average is a particular linear combination.

Recognition clue

If the question explicitly says the population is normal, a small n does not prevent the normal model for X.

Symbols

μ is population mean; σ² population variance; n sample size; σ²/n variance of the mean.

Common mistake

The SE is the square root of σ²/n: σ/√n, not σ²/n and not σ/n.

First confirm three things: random sample, independent observations, and a normal population.
Knowledge check

Marks are normal with variance 36, so σ=6. For an independent random sample of n=25, SE=6/√25=1.2 and the sample-mean distribution is exactly normal.

Central Limit Theorem

For independent observations from a random sample with finite mean μ and SD σ, the sampling distribution of X becomes approximately normal as n grows. The population does not change shape; the distribution of the statistic does.

Normal population

Exactly normal for every valid n when the sampling design is random and observations are independent.

Fairly symmetric

Around 15 or more may be enough under the course guideline.

Most populations

Around 30 or more is a practical course guideline; severe skewness or outliers demand caution.

small n: rougher, wider
medium n
large n: near normal, narrow

Centre

μX=μ; larger n does not move the target.

Spread

σX=σ/√n; larger n concentrates means more tightly.

Recognition clue

Non-normal population + question about an average + sufficiently large n points to the CLT.

Common mistake

Do not say the raw population becomes normal and do not require the CLT when the population is already normal.

Not magical: source slides use n>30; tutorial practice uses n=30. Treat “about 30” as a rule of thumb rather than a theorem boundary.

Official restaurant waiting-time example

Random object: mean wait X for n=36 diners.
Model: CLT gives an approximately normal sampling distribution even if individual waits are not normal.
Centre and SE: μ=8 min; SE=3/√36=.5 min.
Event: P(7.8<X<8.2), a between region.
Standardise: zL=−.4 and zU=.4.
Result: Φ(.4)−Φ(−.4)=.6554−.3446=.3108. About 31.08% of samples of 36 have a mean wait between 7.8 and 8.2 minutes.
Knowledge check: what becomes approximately normal?

The sampling distribution of X, not the original population and not each observation.

Sample-Mean Probabilities

Standardising measures how many standard errors a possible sample mean lies from μ. Once the boundary is on the Z scale, the normal CDF supplies area.

Z = X − μσ√n

Symbols: X sample mean; μ population mean; σ population SD; n sample size; Z standardised distance.

Conditions: a random independent sample and either a normal population or a justified CLT approximation.

below: Φ(z)
above: 1−Φ(z)
between: Φ(zU)−Φ(zL)
Identify: name the random object and decide X or X.
Model: state normal-population or CLT justification.
Parameters: write μ, σ, n, then centre μ and SE σ/√n.
Event: translate words into a symbolic region before calculating.
Standardise: calculate boundary Z-value(s) without early rounding.
Area: apply left, right, between or inverse logic and interpret in context.
If the sentence says “one person/item/time,” use X and σ. If it says “average of n,” use X and σ/√n.
Knowledge check: what is the first step for “the mean exceeds 72”?

Write the event P(X>72) and mark it as a right tail before computing the Z-value.

Inverse and Central-Interval Problems

A forward problem supplies a boundary and asks for area. An inverse problem supplies area and asks for the boundary. Back-transform with boundary=centre+z·SE.

Boundary formulas

XL=μ+zLσ√n

XU=μ+zUσ√n

Not a confidence interval

μ is fixed and known here. We are finding an interval containing a stated fraction of possible sample means, not estimating an unknown parameter.

inverse: area → z → boundary
central 90%: 5% each tail
central 95%: 2.5% each tail
Normal tables and inverse-normal functions use cumulative area to the left. “65% above” therefore becomes .35 to the left.

Official central 95% example

Model: sample means have centre μ=368 and SE=15/√25=3.
Area: 95% central leaves 5% outside, so 2.5% lies in each tail.
Z boundaries: cumulative areas .025 and .975 give zL=−1.96 and zU=1.96.
Back-transform: 368±1.96(3)=362.12 and 373.88.
Interpret: 95% of all sample means from samples of 25 fall in this interval.
Knowledge check: above which boundary do 10% of means lie?

Ten percent above means .90 below. z=.90 quantile≈1.2816, so boundary=μ+1.2816·SE.

12

Sampling Distribution of the Sample Proportion

A sample proportion is a mean of zero-one indicators: each sampled unit contributes 1 for “has the characteristic” and 0 otherwise. That connection explains both its centre π and its binomial-based standard error.

Model and notation

p=X/n, where X is the success count; 0≤p≤1.

μp=π,   σp=π(1−π)n

Z=p−πσp

Approximation conditions

Check nπ≥5 and n(1−π)≥5. These ensure enough expected successes and failures for the normal approximation.

Known π belongs in the theoretical SE. If π is not supplied and the task says estimate the SE, use observed p.

Recognition clue

Words such as percentage, fraction, yes/no or support rate indicate p.

Common mistakes

Using 40 instead of .40; skipping both approximation checks; replacing known π with observed p.

Write the two expected counts nπ and n(1−π) on separate lines. Both must meet the course threshold.

Official voter-proportion example

Random object: p, the proportion supporting Proposition A in n=200.
Conditions: nπ=80 and n(1−π)=120, so normal approximation is appropriate.
Centre and SE: π=.40; SE=√(.4·.6/200)=.034641.
Event: P(.40≤p≤.45), a between region.
Z values: 0 and 1.4434.
Result: full precision gives .4255; the source rounds z to 1.44 and reports .4251. Both are documented; avoid early rounding when technology permits.
Knowledge check: known π or observed p?

If π=.70 is supplied, use .70 in the theoretical SE even when p=.75. If π is unknown and p=.35 is observed, estimate with √(.35·.65/n).

Difference Between Two Independent Sample Means

Treat the difference as one new random variable. Its centre is the difference between population means, not automatically zero; its variance adds the two independent mean variances.

Conditions

Two random samples; independence within and between samples; both populations normal or both sample sizes sufficiently large (course guideline n≥30); population SDs known.

μX₁−X₂=μ₁−μ₂

SE=σ₁²n₁ + σ₂²n₂

Z=(X₁−X₂)−(μ₁−μ₂)SE

14

Finite-Population Correction

Sampling without replacement creates dependence: once a unit is selected it cannot appear again. When the sample is a substantial fraction of a finite population, that extra information reduces uncertainty.

Do I need the finite-population correction?

  1. Sampling without replacement? If no, stop: no FPC.
  2. Finite population size N known? If no, stop.
  3. Is n/N>.05? If yes, apply it.

Factor

FPC=N−nN−1

Mean SE

σ√n×FPC

Proportion SE

π(1−π)n×FPC

Effect

FPC≤1, so it can only reduce or leave unchanged the standard error; it never moves the centre.

Common mistake

Applying it with replacement or forgetting the outer square root.

Write “without replacement?” and compute n/N before touching the SE formula.
Knowledge check: N=500, n=20 without replacement

n/N=.04≤.05, so the course rule says no FPC is needed.

Question 2 — the X versus X̄ exam trap

The population of marks is normal with μ=78 and variance 36, so σ=√36=6.

Part (a) names one student; part (b) names an average of 50 students. Only an average uses σ/√n.

(a) One individual X

Random object: X, one mark. Model: X∼N(78,36). Spread: σ=6, not a standard error. Event: P(X<75), left tail. Z: (75−78)/6=−.5. Probability: Φ(−.5)=.3085. About 30.85% of individual marks are below 75.

(b) Sample average X

Random object: X for n=50. Model: exact normal sampling distribution. Centre: 78. SE: 6/√50=.848528. Event: P(X<75), left tail. Z: −3.5355. Probability: .0002. A class mean below 75 is far rarer than one mark below 75.

Question 3 — exponential observation versus CLT mean

Unit bridge: 400 calls per hour means an average interarrival time of 1/400 hour = 3600/400=9 seconds. For an exponential distribution, population mean and SD are both 1/rate, so μ=σ=9 seconds.

(a) One waiting time X

Random object/model: one exponential waiting time, not a mean and not normal. Event: P(X>12), right tail. Exponential survival gives e−12/9=.2636. About 26.36% of single gaps exceed 12 seconds.

(b) Mean of 30 waiting times

Random object: X. Approximation: CLT for n=30 under the course rule. Centre: 9 seconds. SE: 9/√30=1.643168. Event: P(X>12), right tail. Z: 1.8257. Probability: 1−Φ(1.8257)=.0339. Only about 3.39% of 30-gap averages exceed 12 seconds.

The exponential model is used only for the single observation in (a); the CLT mean model is used in (b). All quantities remain in seconds.

Questions 10–11 — unbiasedness and sample size

Q10: E(X)=E[(X₁+…+Xₙ)/n]=(nμ)/n=μ. Across repeated random samples, over- and under-estimates balance. Unbiased does not mean every sample mean equals μ.

Q11: SE=σ/√n decreases because √n grows. Intuitively, averaging more independent observations lets positive and negative deviations offset; the centre stays μ.

Question 14 — finite population correction
Recognise: sampling is without replacement from finite N=200.
Decide: n/N=16/200=.08>.05, so apply the FPC.
Build the spread: uncorrected SE=.04/√16=.0100; FPC=√[(200−16)/(200−1)]=√(184/199)=.9616; corrected SE=.0100(.9616)=.0096157.

The event 1.31<X<1.33 is a between region centred relative to μ=1.30. Lower z=(1.31−1.30)/.0096157=1.0400; upper z=(1.33−1.30)/.0096157=3.1199. Therefore P=Φ(3.1199)−Φ(1.0400)=.1483. About 14.83% of such samples have a mean in this interval.

The FPC multiplies the standard error only. It does not change μ or the original bounds.

Question-Recognition Decision Tree

  1. One observation, average, proportion, or difference of averages?
  2. Name the random statistic and its mean.
  3. Write its SE before inserting numbers.
  4. Normal population? If yes, mean model is exact. If no, is n sufficiently large?
  5. Proportion? Check nπ≥5 and n(1−π)≥5.
  6. Two samples? Fix population 1 and 2; check independence and known σ values.
  7. Without replacement from finite N? If n/N>.05, apply FPC.
  8. Translate below/above/between/inverse into a region.
  9. Standardise, calculate, then interpret the probability in context.

Calculator-neutral Normal CDF and inverse guide

  1. Left tail: Φ(z) is the cumulative area to the left of z.
  2. Right tail: calculate 1−Φ(z). Do not report Φ(z) when the words say “above”.
  3. Between: calculate Φ(zupper)−Φ(zlower).
  4. Inverse: first express the requested boundary as cumulative area to the left, then use the inverse Normal function to obtain z.
  5. Central area C: each tail has area (1−C)/2, so the positive critical z has left cumulative area (1+C)/2.
  6. Precision: retain full calculator precision for SE and z; normally round only the final probability or boundary to four decimals.

One-Page Final Revision Sheet

Recognise

One value X → σ. Average X → σ/√n. Proportion p → √[π(1−π)/n]. Two averages → √(σ₁²/n₁+σ₂²/n₂).

Centres

μX=μ; μp=π; μdiff=μ₁−μ₂.

Conditions

Mean: normal population exact; otherwise CLT around n≈30, sometimes n≈15 if fairly symmetric. Proportion: nπ≥5 and n(1−π)≥5. Difference: random independent samples; normal or both large; known σ. FPC: without replacement, finite N, n/N>.05.

Tails

Below=Φ(z). Above=1−Φ(z). Between=Φ(upper)−Φ(lower). Inverse: convert requested area to left-tail, find z, back-transform.

Core formulas

Z mean=(X−μ)/(σ/√n). Z proportion=(p−π)/√[π(1−π)/n]. Z diff=[diff−(μ₁−μ₂)]/SE. FPC=√[(N−n)/(N−1)].

Interpret

“There is probability ___ that a random sample of size ___ produces [named statistic and event].”

Last checks

Variance or SD? Decimal or percent? Correct tail? Probability in [0,1]? SE smaller for larger n? Did you preserve group order and interpret?
