    8.1 · Point Estimates, Interval Estimates & Good Estimators

    Objective: Distinguish a population parameter from its sample estimator, distinguish point from interval estimation, and explain unbiasedness, consistency and relative efficiency in the terminology used by the Chapter 8 slides.

    Recognise

    • The question asks what one sample statistic estimates.
    • The question contrasts a single estimate with a range.
    • The question asks which estimator is unbiased, consistent or relatively more efficient.

    Explanation

    A population parameter is an unknown population characteristic such as the mean μ or proportion π. A sample statistic such as X̄ or p is used to estimate it.

    A point estimate is one number. A confidence interval adds information about precision by giving lower and upper confidence limits around a point estimate.

    An unbiased estimator has expected value equal to the population parameter. A consistent estimator gets closer to the parameter as sample size increases. Among two unbiased estimators, the one with smaller variance is relatively more efficient.

    Formula / decision object: Population mean μ ← estimated by X̄; population proportion π ← estimated by p. Point estimate = one statistic; interval estimate = point estimate plus quantified uncertainty.

    Excel route

    1. No special Excel function is needed for these definitions. In workbook questions, identify the statistic you are calculating before building any formula.
    2. If raw values are supplied and the point estimate is a mean, AVERAGE(range) produces X̄; if the point estimate is a proportion, compute successes / n.

    Say aloud: “The parameter belongs to the population; the estimator comes from the sample. A point estimate is one number. A confidence interval adds uncertainty. Unbiased means centred correctly, consistent means improving with larger n, and relative efficiency means smaller variance among unbiased estimators.”

    Worked example · Source estimation-process diagram

    1. The population mean μ is unknown.
    2. A random sample is drawn from the population.
    3. The sample mean X̄=50 is the point estimate of μ.
    4. An interval such as 40 to 60 adds a quantified range around the estimate.

    Result: X̄ is the point estimator; the confidence interval is the interval estimate for μ.

    Verify: Do not describe a confidence interval as an interval for individual observations.

    Common trap: Do not swap the population parameter with the sample statistic, and do not treat a confidence interval for a parameter as a range for individual observations.
    Exit criterion: You can name the parameter, its estimator, point versus interval estimation, and all three estimator characteristics without looking.

    Sources: Chapter 8 slides pp. 4–9, especially pp. 5–7 and p. 13 · Chapter 8 slide objectives/outline pp. 2–3

    8.2 · Confidence Level, α, Margin of Error & Correct Interpretation

    Objective: Understand what a confidence interval says, connect confidence level to α and α/2, identify margin of error, and avoid the fixed-interval probability mistake.

    Recognise

    • The question asks what “95% confidence” means.
    • The question asks for α or α/2 from a confidence level.
    • The question asks what widens or narrows an interval, or what the margin of error represents.

    Explanation

    The Chapter 8 source gives the general structure: point estimate ± (critical value)(standard error). The amount added and subtracted is the margin of error.

    For a 95% confidence level, 1−α=0.95, so α=0.05 and α/2=0.025 in each tail for a two-sided interval.

    The long-run interpretation is procedural: 95% of intervals constructed in this way would contain the unknown parameter. Once one particular interval has been calculated, the fixed parameter either lies in it or it does not.

    Formula / decision object: Confidence interval = Point estimate ± Margin of error, where Margin of error = Critical value × Standard error.

    Excel route

    1. Excel may calculate the critical value and standard error, but you must still identify the confidence coefficient, α and whether the interval is two-sided.
    2. For a 95% two-sided normal interval, the source uses zα/2=1.96.

    Say aloud: “Confidence describes the long-run interval procedure, not a probability that the fixed parameter moves around. For 95%, alpha is 0.05 and each tail gets 0.025.”

    Worked example · Cereal-fill confidence interpretation

    1. The source considers repeated samples of the same size.
    2. Each sample gives a different X̄ and therefore a different interval.
    3. Most intervals produced by the 95% procedure contain μ; some do not.
    4. In practice only one sample is observed, so we report that we are 95% confident that its interval contains μ.

    Result: Confidence is a property of the repeated procedure used to construct the interval.

    Verify: Never reinterpret a confidence interval as the range containing 95% of individual data values.

    Common trap: Do not say there is a 95% probability that the fixed parameter lies in the one interval already computed, and do not say 95% of individuals lie inside the CI.
    Exit criterion: You can move from confidence level → α → α/2 and can give the correct long-run interpretation aloud.

    Sources: Chapter 8 slides pp. 8–21, especially pp. 14–20 · Tutorial 8 Q3 (confidence-interval interpretation)

    8.3 · Confidence Interval for μ when Population σ is Known

    Objective: Recognise the known-σ mean interval, check the source assumptions, calculate zα/2, standard error, margin of error and limits, then interpret the interval in context.

    Recognise

    • The target parameter is the population mean μ.
    • The population standard deviation σ is explicitly known.
    • The population is normal, or the source rule permits a large sample when it is not normal.

    Explanation

    When σ is known, the source uses the standard normal critical value zα/2 and standard error σ/√n.

    The source states the assumptions as: σ known; population normally distributed; if the population is not normal, use a large sample n>30.

    Higher confidence uses a larger critical value and therefore a wider interval. Larger n reduces standard error and narrows the interval.

    Formula / decision object: X̄ ± zα/2(σ/√n)

    Excel route

    1. Build the standard error as =sigma/SQRT(n).
    2. For a two-sided confidence level stored as CL, obtain a positive critical value with =NORM.S.INV(1-(1-CL)/2) or use the equivalent source-style absolute value of NORM.S.INV((1-CL)/2).
    3. Lower = Xbar − z*SE; Upper = Xbar + z*SE.

    Say aloud: “Mean parameter, sigma known: use Z. Standard error is sigma over root n. Interval is X-bar plus or minus z alpha over two times that standard error.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 8 Q1: X̄=85, σ=8, n=64, 95% confidence

    1. Target: μ. Population σ is known, so select the Z interval.
    2. 95% confidence gives zα/2=1.96.
    3. SE=σ/√n=8/√64=1.
    4. Margin=1.96×1=1.96.
    5. Limits: 85−1.96=83.04 and 85+1.96=86.96.

    Result: 95% CI: (83.04, 86.96).

    Verify: The interval is centred at X̄=85 and is symmetric because the same margin is added and subtracted.

    Common trap: Do not use Student t merely because a sample is involved: when population sigma is explicitly known, the source route is Z. Also do not forget the square root of n in the standard error.
    Exit criterion: You can recognise known σ instantly and calculate the complete interval from confidence level to interpretation without prompts.

    Sources: Chapter 8 slides pp. 17–24 · Tutorial 8 Q1, Q2 and Q4 · A1S2 2026 formula sheet: population mean, σ known inference row

    8.4 · Student t Distribution, Degrees of Freedom & the Supplied t Table

    Objective: Explain why Student t replaces Z when σ is unknown, calculate df=n−1, understand the shape of the t family, and read the exact supplied t table correctly.

    Recognise

    • Population σ is unknown and sample s is used instead.
    • A critical t value is requested from confidence level and n.
    • The question gives a t table and asks you to locate a row/column.

    Explanation

    Replacing unknown σ with sample s introduces extra uncertainty because s varies from sample to sample. The source therefore uses Student t.

    Student t is a family of symmetric bell-shaped distributions with fatter tails than the standard normal. Its critical value depends on degrees of freedom, df=n−1.

    The supplied table contains t values in the body. Choose the df row, then use the one-tail/two-tail probability heading that matches the confidence interval. As df grows, t approaches Z.

    Formula / decision object: df = n−1; for a two-sided (1−α) confidence interval use the t critical value with upper-tail area α/2.

    Excel route

    1. The Chapter 8 Excel example uses =T.INV.2T(1-confidence_level, df) to obtain the positive two-sided critical t value.
    2. For a 95% interval and n=100, df=99 and the source uses t≈1.9842.

    Say aloud: “Sigma unknown means I pay for the extra uncertainty with Student t. Degrees of freedom are n minus one. For a two-sided confidence interval I use alpha over two in each tail.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 8 Q9(a): 95% confidence, n=10

    1. Compute df=n−1=9.
    2. 95% confidence gives α=.05 and α/2=.025.
    3. Go to df=9 in the supplied t table.
    4. Read the one-tail .025 / cumulative .975 column.
    5. The table gives t=2.262.

    Result: t critical = 2.262.

    Verify: The table body contains t values, not probabilities; the probability is selected from the heading.

    Common trap: Do not use df=n, and do not read a probability out of the body of the supplied t table. The body contains t values; choose the correct df row and tail-probability column.
    Exit criterion: You can read the exact supplied t table without guessing a column and can explain why t approaches Z as df increases.

    8.5 · Confidence Interval for μ when Population σ is Unknown

    Objective: Construct and interpret a mean confidence interval when σ is unknown using s, Student t and df=n−1, including the source-supported normality check and Excel route.

    Recognise

    • The target is μ.
    • Population σ is unknown; sample standard deviation s is supplied.
    • The source asks you to assume/check an approximately normal population for the t interval.

    Explanation

    Use the t interval X̄ ± tα/2(s/√n). The centre is still X̄, but the critical value comes from Student t with df=n−1.

    The source says interpreting the interval requires an approximately normal population and suggests checking this with a normal probability plot or boxplot.

    A confidence interval for μ estimates the population mean, not the range of individual observations. That is why an individual value may lie outside the CI without being unusual.

    Formula / decision object: X̄ ± tα/2,df (s/√n), with df=n−1.

    Excel route

    1. SE: =s/SQRT(n).
    2. df: =n-1.
    3. Critical t: =T.INV.2T(1-confidence_level,df).
    4. Half width = t*SE; lower = Xbar-halfwidth; upper = Xbar+halfwidth.

    Say aloud: “Mean parameter, sigma unknown: use s and Student t with n minus one degrees of freedom. The interval estimates the population mean, not individual observations.”

    Worked example · Source travel-time example: n=100, X̄=110.27, s=28.95, 95% confidence

    1. σ is unknown, so use Student t.
    2. df=99; the source gives tα/2=1.9842.
    3. SE=28.95/√100=2.895.
    4. Margin=1.9842×2.895≈5.7443.
    5. Limits are approximately 104.53 and 116.01.

    Result: 95% CI: 104.53 ≤ μ ≤ 116.01.

    Verify: The interval is for μ. The source separately reminds you to assess approximate normality.

    Common trap: Do not switch back to Z just because n looks large when population sigma is unknown in this Chapter 8 route; use sample s and Student t with df=n-1. The interval estimates mu, not an individual value.
    Exit criterion: You can choose t from the wording, determine df, calculate the interval, and explain why the CI is about μ rather than one tyre or one observation.

    8.6 · Confidence Interval for the Population Proportion π

    Objective: Use the sample proportion p to estimate π, check the source normal-approximation condition, calculate the estimated standard error and construct/interpret a z confidence interval.

    Recognise

    • The variable is binary/categorical and the target is a population proportion π.
    • You are given successes x and sample size n, or sample p and n.
    • The task is a confidence interval rather than a hypothesis test.

    Explanation

    Compute p=x/n. For interval estimation, the source estimates the standard error with √[p(1−p)/n].

    The Chapter 8 slide states the large-sample condition as np>5 and n(1−p)>5.

    The confidence interval is p ± zα/2√[p(1−p)/n]. Interpret the limits as plausible values for the population proportion π under the confidence procedure.

    Formula / decision object: p ± zα/2 √[p(1−p)/n]

    Excel route

    1. Sample proportion: =successes/n.
    2. Critical z: =NORM.S.INV(1-(1-confidence_level)/2).
    3. SE: =SQRT(p*(1-p)/n).
    4. Half width = z*SE; lower/upper = p ± half width.

    Say aloud: “For a proportion interval, p estimates pi. I use p inside the estimated standard error, check np and n one-minus-p, then use the Z critical value.”

    Worked example · Source left-handed example: 25 of 100, 95% confidence

    1. p=25/100=.25.
    2. Check np=25>5 and n(1−p)=75>5.
    3. SE=√(.25×.75/100)=.0433.
    4. Margin=1.96×.0433≈.0849.
    5. Limits: .25−.0849=.1651 and .25+.0849=.3349.

    Result: 95% CI for π: (0.1651, 0.3349).

    Verify: The interval is for the population proportion, not for 95% of people.

    Common trap: Do not use a null-hypothesis proportion inside a confidence-interval standard error. For the Chapter 8 proportion CI, use the observed sample p in the estimated SE.
    Exit criterion: You can compute p, verify the condition, build the interval and state the interpretation in terms of π.

    8.7 · Required Sample Size for a Population Mean

    Objective: Determine the required sample size for estimating μ at a chosen confidence and margin of error, understand the role of σ, and always round the final requirement upward.

    Recognise

    • The question asks “how large a sample” or “minimum required n”.
    • The target is a population mean.
    • A margin of error e, confidence level and σ (or an estimate of σ) are supplied.

    Explanation

    The source defines margin of error e as the amount added and subtracted to the point estimate. Starting from e=zα/2σ/√n and solving for n gives n=z²σ²/e².

    To determine n you need the confidence level, acceptable margin of error and σ. If σ is unknown, the source says use a conservative anticipated value or estimate σ from a pilot sample.

    Required sample size is always rounded upward because rounding downward would fail to guarantee the requested precision.

    Formula / decision object: n = zα/2² σ² / e²; always round up.

    Excel route

    1. Critical z from confidence level with NORM.S.INV.
    2. Calculated n can be built as =(z*sigma/e)^2.
    3. Required n: =ROUNDUP(calculated_n,0).

    Say aloud: “Sample size for a mean comes from the margin-of-error equation. Smaller error or higher confidence needs a larger n, and I always round the required n up.”

    Worked example · Source download-time example: σ=25, e=5, 95% confidence

    1. 95% gives z=1.96.
    2. n=(1.96×25/5)².
    3. n=96.04.
    4. Round upward.

    Result: Required n=97.

    Verify: If you report 96, the requested maximum margin of error is no longer guaranteed.

    Common trap: Never round a required sample size down or to the nearest integer. Round the final requirement upward. If sigma is unknown, use only the source-supported pilot/conservative estimate approach.
    Exit criterion: You can derive/select the mean sample-size formula, explain each input and round up automatically.

    Sources: Chapter 8 slides pp. 45–52 · Tutorial 8 Q5–Q6 · A1S2 2026 formula sheet sample-size formula for a mean

    8.8 · Required Sample Size for a Population Proportion

    Objective: Determine required n for estimating π, decide whether to use a pilot/prior estimate or the conservative 0.5 value, and round upward.

    Recognise

    • The target is a population proportion.
    • The question asks for required/minimum n at a stated confidence and margin e.
    • A prior/pilot proportion may be supplied; if none is supplied, the course uses 0.5 conservatively.

    Explanation

    The source formula is n=z²π(1−π)/e². When π is unknown, a pilot estimate can be used.

    If no prior estimate exists, the course uses π=.5 because .5(1−.5)=.25 is the largest possible Bernoulli variance term and therefore gives the conservative largest required sample size.

    As with mean sample size, higher confidence or smaller margin of error requires a larger sample, and final n is rounded upward.

    Formula / decision object: n = zα/2² π(1−π) / e²; if no prior π is available, use 0.5; always round up.

    Excel route

    1. Use a pilot/prior proportion in the formula if the question supplies one.
    2. If none is supplied, enter 0.5.
    3. Required n: =ROUNDUP((z^2*p*(1-p))/e^2,0).

    Say aloud: “For proportion sample size I need confidence, margin and a value for pi. If no estimate exists, I use 0.5 because it is conservative, then I round n up.”

    Worked example · A1S2 Practice Test Q5 style: 95% confidence, e=.06, no prior π

    1. No prior estimate is available, so use π=.5.
    2. 95% gives z=1.96.
    3. n=(1.96²×.5×.5)/.06².
    4. n≈266.7778.
    5. Round upward.

    Result: Required n=267.

    Verify: Do not round 266.7778 to the nearest whole number; required n uses upward rounding.

    Common trap: Use a supplied pilot/prior proportion when one is given; use 0.5 only when no estimate is available. Round the final required n upward.
    Exit criterion: You can decide pilot π versus 0.5, calculate n and explain why upward rounding is mandatory.

    8.9 · Finite Population Correction for Confidence Intervals

    Objective: Recognise when sampling without replacement from finite N requires the course FPC rule, understand why the correction reduces standard error, and apply it to mean/proportion intervals.

    Recognise

    • Sampling is without replacement from a finite population of size N.
    • The sampling fraction n/N must be checked.
    • The source uses the FPC when n/N>0.05; below 5% its practical effect is little or none.

    Explanation

    The finite-population source says use the correction factor when more than 5% of the population is sampled without replacement.

    The factor √[(N−n)/(N−1)] multiplies the usual standard error. Because it is at most 1, it reduces the standard error and therefore narrows the confidence interval.

    The same correction multiplies the mean standard error s/√n and the proportion standard error √[p(1−p)/n].

    Formula / decision object: FPC = √[(N−n)/(N−1)]. Mean CI: X̄ ± t(s/√n)FPC. Proportion CI: p ± z√[p(1−p)/n]FPC.

    Excel route

    1. FPC: =SQRT((N-n)/(N-1)).
    2. Multiply the ordinary SE by the FPC before multiplying by the critical value.
    3. Do not apply the correction merely because N is given; first check sampling without replacement and n/N.

    Say aloud: “Finite population means without replacement. I check n over N. If it is more than five percent, the FPC multiplies and reduces the usual standard error.”

    Worked example · Finite-population source Example 8.10: N=200, n=27, X̄=45.2222, s=23.1472, 95%

    1. Sampling is without replacement and n/N=27/200=.135>0.05, so FPC matters.
    2. df=26 and the source uses t=2.0555.
    3. FPC=√[(200−27)/(200−1)].
    4. Apply t×(s/√n)×FPC as the margin.
    5. The source obtains limits 36.6846 and 53.7598.

    Result: 95% CI: (36.6846, 53.7598).

    Verify: Because FPC<1, the interval is narrower than the corresponding no-FPC interval.

    Common trap: Do not apply FPC merely because N appears in the question. The source requires sampling without replacement and uses n/N>0.05 as the practical trigger. Multiply the standard error by the FPC; do not add it.
    Exit criterion: You can recognise the FPC trigger, write the factor from memory and explain why it narrows the interval.

    8.10 · Finite-Population Sample Size Determination

    Objective: Compute the initial infinite/large-population sample size n₀, adjust it for finite N, and round the resulting required sample size upward.

    Recognise

    • The question asks for required sample size and gives finite N.
    • Sampling is without replacement.
    • You first use the ordinary mean/proportion sample-size formula to obtain n₀.

    Explanation

    The finite-population source tells you to calculate n₀ without FPC first, using the ordinary mean or proportion sample-size formula.

    Formula / decision object: n = n₀N / [n₀ + (N−1)], where n₀ is the unadjusted required sample size.

    8.11 · Bootstrapping: Why, Resampling Logic & Percentile Confidence Limits

    Objective: Explain when bootstrapping is appropriate, distinguish the original sample from bootstrap resamples, preserve resample size n, build the resampling distribution and obtain confidence limits using the course ordered-array method.

    Recognise

    • The population cannot reasonably be assumed normal.
    • You have an observed sample and can resample from it with replacement.
    • The task asks for a bootstrap confidence interval for a population mean.

    Explanation

    The supplied bootstrapping source says that when one cannot assume a normal population, bootstrap methods use repeated resampling with replacement from the initial sample and make no assumption about the underlying population distribution.

    Select the original random sample without replacement from the population. Then each bootstrap resample selects n values with replacement from that observed sample, so values may repeat and others may be omitted.

    Compute X̄ for each resample, repeat many times, order all resampled means, then cut off α/2 from each tail to obtain the lower and upper confidence limits.

    Formula / decision object: Original sample → resample n values WITH replacement → compute X̄* → repeat m times → order X̄* values → take the course-specified α/2 tail cutoffs.

    Excel route

    2. Each resample mean can be calculated with AVERAGE(resample_range).
    3. After all means are calculated, order them or use SMALL/LARGE according to the course cutoff convention for that m and confidence level.

    Say aloud: “Bootstrap resampling happens from the observed sample, with replacement, and every resample stays size n. I calculate the statistic for each resample, order those statistics and cut alpha over two from each tail.”

    Worked example · Source 90% bootstrap example with 100 resample means

    2. There are 100 ordered resample means.
    3. .05×100=5, so the source uses the 5th smallest and 5th largest values.
    4. The 5th smallest is 36.6296 and the 5th largest is 51.5926.

    Result: 90% bootstrap CI: (36.6296, 51.5926).

    Verify: Do not use the minimum and maximum; the course removes α/2 from each tail.

    Common trap: Bootstrap resampling is from the observed sample WITH replacement and keeps resample size n. Do not sort the original raw observations and call those the bootstrap confidence limits.

    7.1 · Population → Sample → Statistic → Sampling Distribution

    Objective: Build the core mental model: a sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a statistic over all possible random samples of a fixed size, not the raw observations in one sample.

    Recognise

    • The question asks what a sampling distribution is.
    • The question contrasts a population, one sample, one statistic and repeated possible statistics.
    • The wording asks why two random samples can produce different means or proportions.

    Explanation

    A population is the full collection of units. A sample is one subset drawn from it. A parameter such as μ, σ or π belongs to the population; a statistic such as X̄ or p is calculated from a sample.

    Different random samples usually contain different observations, so the statistic changes from sample to sample. The sampling distribution describes that variability mathematically.

    The Chapter 7 objective is not merely to calculate one sample statistic; it is to reason about the distribution of the statistic before the sample is observed.

    Formula / decision object: Sampling distribution = distribution of all possible values of a sample statistic for a fixed n. Random-object question: X, X̄, p, or X̄₁−X̄₂?

    Excel route

    1. Excel can calculate a realised sample statistic, but it does not decide what the random object is for you.
    2. Before typing a function, name the statistic whose repeated values form the sampling distribution.

    Say aloud: “One sample gives me one statistic. A sampling distribution is the distribution of that statistic across all possible random samples of the same size.”

    Worked example · Chapter 7 GPA thought experiment

    1. Imagine repeatedly selecting 50 students from the same college.
    2. Each sample produces a possibly different sample mean GPA.
    3. Collect the possible sample means rather than the 50 raw GPAs from one sample.
    4. The distribution of those possible means is the sampling distribution of X̄.

    Result: The random object is X̄, and the sampling distribution is the probability model for its possible values.

    Verify: Do not call one realised set of 50 GPAs a sampling distribution.

    Common trap: Do not confuse population distribution, one sample, one realised statistic and the sampling distribution of that statistic.
    Exit criterion: You can identify the population, sample, parameter, statistic and sampling distribution in a new scenario without looking.

    7.2 · Exact Sampling Distributions & Unbiasedness

    Objective: Construct an exact sampling distribution for a tiny population and use it to see why X̄ is an unbiased estimator of μ.

    Recognise

    • A tiny finite population is listed explicitly.
    • The task says “all possible samples” or asks you to construct the sampling distribution exactly.
    • The question asks why the sample mean is unbiased.

    Explanation

    The source builds a toy population 18,20,22,24 and lists every ordered sample of size 2 with replacement. Each possible sample has its own X̄.

    An exact sampling distribution combines repeated statistic values with their probabilities. Its mean is the expected value of the statistic.

    When E(X̄)=μ, X̄ is an unbiased estimator of μ. Unbiased does not mean every sample mean equals μ.

    Formula / decision object: μ_X̄ = Σ X̄·P(X̄). Unbiasedness: E(X̄)=μ.

    Excel route

    1. For a small enumeration, Excel can list sample combinations and use AVERAGE for each sample, but the key idea is the complete set of possible statistic values.
    2. For Tutorial 7 Q4, sampling is without replacement, so use combinations rather than duplicating ordered draws as if replacement were allowed.

    Say aloud: “Unbiased means the sampling distribution is centred at the true parameter. One sample mean can miss μ; the expected sample mean still equals μ.”

    Worked example · Source toy population 18,20,22,24

    1. Population mean μ=(18+20+22+24)/4=21.
    2. With replacement and n=2 there are 4²=16 ordered samples.
    3. Their sample means range from 18 to 24 and are no longer uniformly distributed.
    4. The mean of the 16 sample means is 21.

    Result: μ_X̄=21=μ, so X̄ is unbiased for μ.

    Verify: The sampling distribution can have a different shape and smaller spread than the population while keeping the same centre.

    Common trap: Do not interpret unbiasedness as “X̄ equals μ in every sample.” It is a statement about the centre across possible samples.
    Exit criterion: You can enumerate a tiny-sample experiment, construct the statistic distribution and explain E(X̄)=μ.

    7.3 · Standard Error & Why Larger n Narrows the Distribution

    Objective: Treat standard error as the standard deviation of a statistic across possible samples and explain exactly how n changes it.

    Recognise

    • The question compares variability of sample means from different n.
    • The question asks for the standard error of X̄.
    • The question asks why increasing sample size makes estimates more stable.

    Explanation

    Different samples produce different X̄ values. The standard error measures the spread of those possible means.

    For the ordinary sample-mean model (sampling with replacement, or without replacement from an effectively infinite population), σ_X̄=σ/√n. The square root matters: quadrupling n halves the standard error. If sampling is without replacement from a finite population, the FPC in Lesson 7.12 can reduce this spread further.

    Increasing n does not shift the centre μ_X̄=μ. It narrows the distribution around the same target.

    Formula / decision object: SE(X̄)=σ/√n. If n is multiplied by k, SE is divided by √k.

    Excel route

    1. In Excel use =sigma/SQRT(n) when population σ is known.
    2. Do not use =sigma/n. SQRT(n) is the entire reason the gain from sample size has diminishing returns.

    Say aloud: “Standard deviation describes individuals; standard error describes the statistic across samples. Larger n shrinks SE by the square-root rule, but the centre stays μ.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q4 comparison

    1. For the population 1,3,6,7,9,10, all without-replacement n=2 sample means have mean 6.
    2. Their exact sampling-distribution SD is 2.0000.
    3. For all without-replacement n=3 sample means, the centre is still 6.
    4. Their exact sampling-distribution SD is about 1.4142.

    Result: The n=3 sampling distribution has less variability while retaining centre 6.

    Verify: This exact Tutorial 7 Q4 enumeration is without replacement from N=6, so its SDs (2.0000 and 1.4142) already reflect finite-population reduction; they should not be mistaken for the ordinary σ/√n values. The key comparison is still that n=3 varies less than n=2.

    Common trap: Do not divide by n; divide by √n. And do not say larger n changes the population standard deviation σ. Also do not use the uncorrected σ/√n formula to reproduce Tutorial 7 Q4's finite without-replacement enumeration; that case belongs to the FPC bridge.
    Exit criterion: You can distinguish SD from SE and predict the effect of any multiplicative change in n.

    Sources: Chapter 7 slides pp. 9–11 and 15 · Tutorial 7 Q4(c) and Q11

    7.4 · One Observation X versus Sample Mean X̄

    Objective: Identify whether the probability is about one observation or an average before selecting the centre and spread.

    Recognise

    • The wording says one person/item/time/mark versus the average of n observations.
    • A variance may be given and must first be converted to σ.
    • The task asks you to interpret a probability about X̄ in context.

    Explanation

    For one observation X from a normal population, use centre μ and spread σ.

    For a sample mean X̄, use centre μ and spread σ/√n. This distinction is one of the most important Chapter 7 exam traps.

    If variance is supplied, convert it to standard deviation before using either model: σ=√variance.

    Formula / decision object: One X: Z=(X−μ)/σ. Sample mean X̄: Z=(X̄−μ)/(σ/√n).

    Excel route

    1. One normal observation: =NORM.DIST(x,mu,sigma,TRUE).
    2. Sample mean: =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE).

    Say aloud: “I name the random object first. One observation uses sigma. An average uses sigma over square-root n.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q2 — one mark versus class average

    1. Marks are normal with μ=78 and variance 36, so σ=6.
    2. Part (a) asks for one student mark below 75: z=(75−78)/6=−0.5, probability≈0.3085.
    3. Part (b) asks for the mean of n=50 students below 75: SE=6/√50≈0.8485.
    4. z=(75−78)/0.8485≈−3.5355, probability≈0.0002.

    Result: The same boundary 75 has radically different probability because X and X̄ have different spreads.

    Verify: The population did not change; the random object changed.

    Common trap: Do not use σ for a sample mean and do not use σ/√n for a single observation.
    Exit criterion: You can classify X versus X̄ before doing any arithmetic and interpret the result in terms of the correct object.

    7.5 · Sample-Mean Probabilities with Known σ: Left, Between & Right

    Objective: Solve forward probabilities for X̄ when population σ is known, with the tail/region matching the words.

    Recognise

    • A normal population or justified normal sampling model is available.
    • The random object is X̄ and population σ is known.
    • The task asks below, between or above a stated sample-mean boundary.

    Explanation

    For a normal population, the sampling distribution of X̄ is exactly normal for every valid n, with centre μ and SE σ/√n.

    Standardise the sample-mean boundary using the SE, not σ.

    Excel cumulative functions return left area. “Above” therefore requires a complement unless you use an equivalent direct route.

    Formula / decision object: X̄ ~ N(μ,σ²/n), Z=(X̄−μ)/(σ/√n). Left=Φ(z); Between=Φ(zU)−Φ(zL); Right=1−Φ(z).

    Excel route

    1. Direct left: =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE).
    2. Right: =1-NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE). Between: upper cumulative minus lower cumulative.

    Say aloud: “Known sigma and a sample mean means normal Z. I draw the region, use sigma over square-root n, and make Excel’s left-area output match the requested tail.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q1(a–c)

    1. μ=100, σ=10, n=25, so SE=10/5=2.
    2. (a) P(X̄<95): z=−2.5, probability≈0.0062.
    3. (b) P(95<X̄<97.5): zL=−2.5, zU=−1.25, probability≈0.0994.
    4. (c) P(X̄>102.2): z=1.1, probability≈0.1357.

    Result: Left≈0.0062; between≈0.0994; right≈0.1357.

    Verify: Every part uses the same centre and SE; only the region changes.

    7.6 · Inverse Sample-Mean Boundaries & Central Symmetric Limits

    Objective: Work backwards from a probability to the X̄ boundary and construct symmetric probability limits around μ.

    Recognise

    • The probability is given but the sample-mean cutoff is unknown.
    • The wording says “there is a 65% chance X̄ is above what value?”
    • The task asks for limits containing a fixed central percentage of sample means.

    Explanation

    Inverse problems reverse the ordinary workflow: probability → z → original X̄ scale.

    For P(X̄>x)=0.65, the area to the left of x is 0.35, so use z=Φ⁻¹(0.35), not Φ⁻¹(0.65).

    For a central C interval, split α=1−C equally between the two tails and transform both z boundaries back with μ+z·SE.

    Formula / decision object: Boundary = μ + z·σ/√n. Central limits: μ ± z*·σ/√n, where z*=Φ⁻¹((1+C)/2).

    Excel route

    1. Right-tail inverse boundary: =NORM.INV(left_area,mu,sigma/SQRT(n)).
    2. Central limits: =mu ± NORM.S.INV((1+C)/2)*sigma/SQRT(n).

    Say aloud: “Inverse means the probability comes first. I convert the requested area to left-cumulative probability, find z, then return to X-bar units.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q1(d)

    1. μ=100, σ=10, n=25, so SE=2.
    2. P(X̄>x)=0.65 means P(X̄≤x)=0.35.
    3. z=Φ⁻¹(0.35)≈−0.3853.
    4. x=100+(−0.3853)(2)≈99.2294.

    Result: There is a 65% chance that X̄ is above approximately 99.2294.

    Verify: Using 0.65 as the left area would put the boundary above μ and reverse the requested region.

    Common trap: Do not use the requested right-tail probability directly as a cumulative-left probability.
    Exit criterion: You can convert verbal inverse/central-area wording into the correct cumulative probabilities and X̄ boundaries.

    7.7 · Central Limit Theorem: What Becomes Normal & When

    Objective: Use the CLT correctly for non-normal populations while preserving the sampling-distribution centre and SE.

    Recognise

    • The population is not stated to be normal or is explicitly skewed/exponential.
    • The task asks about an average of many observations.
    • You must decide whether the sampling distribution of X̄ is approximately normal.

    Explanation

    The CLT concerns the sampling distribution of X̄. It does not transform the original population or individual observations into normal variables.

    The course guideline says normal population → exact normal X̄ for any valid n; fairly symmetric populations may be adequate around n>15; most distributions around n>30. These are course rules of thumb, not magical theorem cutoffs.

    Under the CLT, the sampling distribution remains centred at μ with SE σ/√n. Only the approximate shape changes toward normal.

    Formula / decision object: For sufficiently large n: X̄ ≈ N(μ,σ²/n), even when the original population is not normal.

    Excel route

    1. Once the CLT justifies the normal sampling model, use =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE) for cumulative-left sample-mean probabilities.
    2. Do not use an exponential CDF on the sample mean merely because each individual observation is exponential.

    Say aloud: “The CLT makes the sampling distribution of the mean approximately normal. It does not make the population normal. The centre stays μ and the SE stays σ over square-root n.”

    Worked example · Chapter 7 restaurant waiting-time example

    5. Φ(0.4)−Φ(−0.4)=0.6554−0.3446=0.3108.

    Result: Approximately 0.3108 of samples of 36 have mean wait between 7.8 and 8.2 minutes.

    Verify: The statement is about sample means, not individual waiting times.

    Common trap: Do not say “n=30 makes the population normal.” Also do not forget to convert the exponential rate to consistent time units before finding μ and σ.

    For a theoretical sampling probability with known π, SE=√[π(1−π)/n]. When an estimated SE is requested and π is unknown, use √[p(1−p)/n].

    Formula / decision object: p=X/n; μp=π; theoretical SE(p)=√[π(1−π)/n]. Estimated SE=√[p(1−p)/n] when estimation context requests it.

    Excel route

    1. Sample p = successes/n.
    2. Theoretical SE: =SQRT(pi*(1-pi)/n). Estimated SE: =SQRT(p*(1-p)/n) only in the appropriate estimation context.

    7.10 · Proportion Probabilities, Between Regions & Inverse Bounds

    Objective: Use the normal sampling model for p to solve forward probabilities, between regions and central inverse bounds, and explain how n changes precision.

    Recognise

    • Known π and n are supplied and the question asks about a sample percentage.
    • The event is above/below/between p boundaries.
    • The probability is given and the task asks for symmetric limits around π.

    Explanation

    Once the approximation conditions pass, standardise p using z=(p−π)/SE with SE based on known π.

    Between probabilities require two standardised boundaries and subtraction of cumulative areas.

    For central C bounds, use z*=Φ⁻¹((1+C)/2) and π±z*SE. Increasing n reduces SE and therefore narrows the distribution/bounds.

    Formula / decision object: Z=(p−π)/√[π(1−π)/n]. Central p bounds: π ± z*√[π(1−π)/n].

    7.11 · Difference Between Two Independent Sample Means

    Objective: Treat X̄₁−X̄₂ as one random variable, preserve group order, combine variances correctly and translate comparison wording into a threshold.

    Recognise

    • Two independent populations/groups are compared using sample averages.
    • Population means, population SDs and sample sizes are supplied.
    • The wording says group 1 exceeds group 2, or exceeds it by more than c.

    Explanation

    For the sourced known-σ independent case, the difference is centred at μ₁−μ₂. It is not automatically centred at zero.

    Independent mean variances add: SE=√(σ₁²/n₁+σ₂²/n₂). Do not add the standard deviations directly.

    Fix the order first. “Group 1 exceeds group 2” means X̄₁−X̄₂>0; “by more than 25” means X̄₁−X̄₂>25.

    Formula / decision object: μdiff=μ₁−μ₂; SEdiff=√(σ₁²/n₁+σ₂²/n₂); Z=[(X̄₁−X̄₂)−(μ₁−μ₂)]/SEdiff.

    Excel route

    1. Compute SE with =SQRT(sigma1^2/n1 + sigma2^2/n2).
    2. For a boundary c, use =1-NORM.DIST(c,mu1-mu2,SE,TRUE) for P(X̄₁−X̄₂>c).

    Say aloud: “I freeze the order one minus two, centre the difference at mu one minus mu two, add variances inside the square root, then translate the wording to a threshold.”

    Worked example · Source MBA starting-salary example

    1. US: μ₁=620000, σ₁=145000, n₁=50; UCT: μ₂=600000, σ₂=183000, n₂=60.
    2. Centre μdiff=20000.
    3. SE=√(145000²/50+183000²/60)≈31283.3822.
    4. Event US sample mean exceeds UCT: X̄₁−X̄₂>0.
    5. z=(0−20000)/31283.3822≈−0.6393; right-tail≈0.7387.

    Result: P(X̄US>X̄UCT)≈0.7387.

    Verify: The centre is +20000 because the order is US−UCT.

    Common trap: Do not reverse the groups because an observed mean is larger, and do not centre the difference at zero unless μ₁−μ₂ actually equals zero.
    Exit criterion: You can preserve order, build the difference model, translate thresholds and calculate a probability independently.

    7.12 · Finite Population Correction: Without-Replacement Bridge

    Objective: Recognise when sampling without replacement from finite N changes the standard error, while preserving the current system’s provisional scope flag for these Chapter 7 skills.

    Recognise

    • A finite population size N is supplied.
    • Sampling is explicitly without replacement.
    • The sampling fraction n/N exceeds 5% under the course rule.

    Explanation

    The Chapter 7 deck includes an online finite-population section: when sampling without replacement and n/N>0.05, multiply the usual SE by FPC=√[(N−n)/(N−1)].

    FPC reduces the spread because sampling a substantial part of a finite population leaves less uncertainty. It does not move the centre.

    The canonical master still marks C7.52–C7.58 PROVISIONAL as a scope safeguard. This Guided Learn lesson teaches the sourced bridge without silently promoting those mastery skills.

    Formula / decision object: FPC=√[(N−n)/(N−1)]. Mean SE=σ/√n × FPC. Proportion SE=√[π(1−π)/n] × FPC.

    Excel route

    1. =SQRT((N-n)/(N-1)) computes the correction factor.
    2. Multiply the ordinary mean/proportion SE by that factor before standardising.

    Say aloud: “Finite population correction needs finite N, sampling without replacement and a meaningful sampling fraction. It narrows the SE; it does not change the centre.”

    Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q14

    1. Population of Ping-Pong balls: μ=1.30, σ=0.04, N=200.
    2. n=16 without replacement, so n/N=0.08>0.05 and FPC applies.
    3. FPC=√(184/199)≈0.96157; SE=.04/4×FPC≈0.009616.
    4. P(1.31<X̄<1.33): zL≈1.03996, zU≈3.11989.
    5. Probability≈0.1483.

    Result: P(1.31<X̄<1.33)≈0.1483 under the sourced FPC route.

    Verify: Without FPC the spread would be slightly larger; the centre remains 1.30 either way.

    Common trap: Do not use FPC with replacement, and do not let this teaching lesson silently convert the existing PROVISIONAL mastery status into ACTIVE evidence.
    Exit criterion: You can identify the FPC trigger, calculate the corrected SE and explain what the correction changes.
