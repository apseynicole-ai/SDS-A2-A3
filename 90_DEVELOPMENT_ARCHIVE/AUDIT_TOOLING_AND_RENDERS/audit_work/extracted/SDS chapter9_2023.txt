===== PAGE 5 =====
Hypothesis Testing
◼ Previously, we sought to determine whether a 
sample mean is consistent with a known 
population mean.
◼ Here, we seek to validate a claim about the 
population using the sample mean.
◼ For this new goal, we use an inferential method 
called: hypothesis testing.
===== PAGE 6 =====
◼ Steps for hypothesis testing:
1) State a straightforward claim about the population 
mean 𝜇 . This claim is referred to as the null 
hypothesis 𝐻0 .
2) State a mutually exclusive claim to the null 
hypothesis. Such a claim is referred to as the 
alternative hypothesis 𝐻1 .
3) Begin with the assumption that 𝐻0 is true.
◼ Recall: innocent until proven guilty!
4) Examine the sample statistics to see if it better 
supports 𝐻0 or 𝐻1.

The Null Hypothesis H0
◼ States the claim or assertion to be validated.
◼ Assumes the status-quo scenario.
◼ Represents the current belief in a situation.
◼ May or may not be rejected.
◼ Note: the null hypothesis is NEVER accepted.
◼ Always about a population parameter NOT 
about a sample statistic.

The Hypothesis Testing Process
◼ Claim: The population mean age is 50.
H0: 𝜇 = 50 H1: 𝜇 ≠ 50
◼ Sample the population and calculate the sample mean.
◼ Suppose the sample mean value ҧ𝑥 = 20.
◼ This is significantly less than the claimed mean population 
age of 50.
◼ If the null hypothesis were true, the probability of getting such 
a different sample mean would be very small, so reject the 
null hypothesis.
◼ In other words, getting a sample mean age of 20 is so 
unlikely if the population mean is 50.
◼ Conclude that the population mean could not have been 50.

Risks in Decision Making Using 
Hypothesis Testing
◼ Type I Error:
◼ Reject a true null hypothesis H0 .
◼ A Type I error is a “false alarm”.
◼ The probability of a Type I error is often denoted by 𝜶.
◼ Called “level of significance” of the test.
◼ Specified by the researcher in advance.
◼ Type II Error:
◼ Failure to reject a false null hypothesis H0 .
◼ Type II error represents a “missed opportunity”.
◼ The probability of a Type II error is often denoted by 𝜷.

𝑝-value Approach to Hypothesis 
Testing
◼ 𝑝-value: probability of obtaining a test statistic equal 
to or more extreme than the observed sample value, 
given that H0 is true.
◼ The 𝑝-value is also called the observed level of 
significance.
◼ It is the smallest value of 𝛼 for which H0 can be rejected.
◼ Interpreting the 𝑝-value: compare the 𝑝-value with 𝛼.
◼ If the 𝑝-value < 𝛼, reject H0.
◼ If the 𝑝-value ≥ 𝛼, do not reject H0.

===== PAGE 70 =====
Statistical Significance vs 
Practical Significance
◼ Statistically significant results (that is, rejecting 
the null hypothesis) are not always of practical 
significance.
◼ This is more likely to happen when the sample size 
gets very large.
◼ Practically important results might be found to 
be statistically insignificant (that is, failing to 
reject the null hypothesis).
◼ This is more likely to happen when the sample size is 
relatively small.
===== PAGE 71 =====
Reporting Findings and Ethical Issues
◼ Should document and report both good and bad results.
◼ Should not just report statistically significant results.
◼ Reports should distinguish between poor research 
methodology and unethical behaviour.
◼ Ethical issues can arise so they must be properly 
handled and reported. These include,
◼ The use of human subjects.
◼ The data collection method.
◼ The type of test being used.
◼ The level of significance being used.
◼ The cleaning and discarding of the data.
◼ The failure to report pertinent findings.
