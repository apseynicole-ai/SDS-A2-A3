Chapter 6 · Continuous distributions
6.0 Diagnostic
Unlabelled recognition across continuous models.
Identify the random object, distribution and region before calculating.
6.1 Continuous Probability Foundations
Probability is area; an exact point has probability zero.
For a continuous random variable, probability is area under its density. Therefore P(X=c)=0, and < versus ≤ does not change the probability.
6.2 Normal Distribution
Notation, parameters, standardisation and shape.
X ∼  N(μ,σ²). Changing μ shifts the curve; changing σ changes spread. Standardise with Z=(X−μ)/σ.
6.3 Normal Regions and Forward Probability
Words → event → region → method → calculation.
Left: Φ(z). Right: 1−Φ(z). Between: Φ(zupper)−Φ(zlower). Draw or identify the region first.
6.4 Inverse Normal and Percentiles
Probability is known; the boundary is unknown.
Convert the requested region to cumulative-left probability, obtain z with NORM.S.INV, then x=μ+zσ. NORM.INV can return x directly.
6.5 Evaluation of Normality
Integrate numerical and graphical evidence.
Compare mean/median/mode, empirical-rule proportions, skewness/kurtosis and suitable plots. No single check proves normality.
6.6 Excel Normality Laboratory
Use the official Steel data and supplied workbooks.
Progress VIEW → COMPLETE → RECONSTRUCT → EXECUTE FROM QUESTION. Sort X; create j; calculate αj=j/(n+1); compute Z with NORM.S.INV; scatterplot Xon y against Z on x; integrate with histogram and descriptive evidence. Statistical reasoning and Excel execution remain separate evidence.
6.7 Uniform Distribution
Constant density between a and b; probability is rectangular area.
f(x)=1/(b−a) on [a,b]. P(c<X<d)=(d−c)/(b−a), μ=(a+b)/2, σ=√[(b−a)²/12].
6.8 Exponential Distribution
Waiting time with rate and time units aligned.
X ∼  exp(λ), E(X)=1/λ, P(X≤x)=1−e^(−λx), P(X≥x)=e^(−λx). Poisson counts; exponential waits.
6.9 Inverse Exponential
Solve for a waiting-time boundary.
For cumulative probability α, xα=−ln(1−α)/λ. This lecturer-supported extension must not be confused with inverse normal.
6.10 Normal Approximation to Binomial
Check conditions, set centre/spread, then continuity-correct.
Use only when nπ>5 and n(1−π)>5. Approximate centre nπ, SD √[nπ(1−π)]. Exact x becomes x−0.5<Xa<x+0.5; ≤x becomes <x+0.5; ≥x becomes >x−0.5.

7.0 Diagnostic
Object-first unlabelled diagnostic.
Ask what is random before selecting a distribution.
7.1 Population → Sample → Statistic → Sampling Distribution
A statistic varies across possible random samples.
A sampling distribution is the distribution of the calculated statistic over all possible samples of fixed size—not the distribution of raw observations in one sample.
7.2 Exact Sampling Distributions and Unbiasedness
Enumerate tiny populations to see long-run centre.
Each possible sample produces a statistic. The distribution of these statistics can show E(X̄ )=μ even though one realised X̄  need not equal μ.
7.3 Standard Error
The spread of a statistic across samples.
σX̄ =σ/√n. Increasing n narrows the sampling distribution without changing its centre.
7.4 X versus X̄
One observation uses σ; an average uses σ/√n.
Underline the random object. X is one individual observation. X̄  is an average of n observations and uses standard error.
7.5 X̄  — σ known / Z
Normal sample-mean probabilities with known population σ.
X̄  ∼  N(μ,σ²/n), Z=(X̄ −μ)/(σ/√n). Use cumulative-left area, complements and inverse functions as in Chapter 6.
7.6 Central Limit Theorem
The sampling distribution of X̄  becomes approximately normal for large enough n.
The original population does not become normal. If the population is normal, X̄  is normal for every valid n; otherwise use the source-aligned “large enough” principle.
7.7 X̄  — σ unknown / Student t
Current 2026 source §2.6: sample-mean Student-t route when population sigma is unknown.

8.1 Point vs Interval Estimation
A point estimate is one statistic; an interval adds a quantified uncertainty range.
Match Xbar to mu and p to pi.
8.2 Estimator Characteristics
Unbiasedness, consistency and relative efficiency are different properties.
Unbiased: expected value equals parameter. Consistent: approaches the parameter as n grows. Relatively efficient: smaller variance among unbiased estimators.
8.3 Confidence, Alpha, Critical Value, Standard Error, Margin of Error
Estimate +/- critical value x standard error.
Confidence describes the long-run interval procedure. A fixed interval either contains the parameter or does not; it is not an interval for individual observations.
8.4 CI for Mu - Sigma Known
Known population sigma selects z.
OFF23 rule: if the population is not normal, use a large sample n > 30. Do not use the rhetorical p. 26 slide as a method-selection rule. Current 2026 Transcript 10 also supportsthe known-sigma → Z route.
8.5 CI for Mu - Sigma Unknown / Student t
Unknown sigma selects sample s, Student t and df=n-1.
Non-listed t-table row convention remains AWAIT_MEMO_OR_CURRENT_SOURCE. Exact independently calculated t values may check arithmetic but are not an officiallecturer table convention. Current 2026 Transcript 10 also supports the unknown-sigma → Student-t route.
8.6 CI for Population Proportion
Use p +/- z*sqrt[p(1-p)/n].
Preserve strict source conditions np > 5 and n(1-p) > 5. Current 2026 Transcripts 10–11 support the sample-proportion centre, SE, approximation checks and confidence-interval structure.
8.7 Required Sample Size - Mean

Chapter 9 · Fundamentals of Hypothesis Testing: One-Sample Tests
9.1 Hypothesis Foundations
Claims, H0/H1, the assumption under H0 and correct decision language.
A hypothesis is about a population parameter. H0 carries equality and is assumed true at the start; H1 is mutually exclusive. Conclude reject H0 or do not reject H0 — never
accept H0.
9.2 Decision Risk: α, β and Power
Type I/II errors, significance, confidence coefficient and power.
α=P(Type I error); β=P(Type II error); confidence coefficient=1−α; power=1−β. For fixed n, lowering α increases β. Greater true-vs-null distance and larger n increase power;larger variance decreases it.
9.3 Tails, α and Rejection Regions
Let H1 determine the tail before calculating.
≠ gives two tails, < gives lower tail, > gives upper tail. A two-tail test splits α/2 into each tail; a one-tail test places all α in the directed tail.
9.4 Mean Test — σ Known: Z Critical-Value Method
Known population σ selects the Z route.
Use Z=(X̄ −μ0)/(σ/√n), determine critical value(s) from α and the tail, then compare Zcalc with the rejection region and conclude in context.
9.5 Z Test — p-Value + Excel
Interpret the p-value under H0, know its source synonyms, reconstruct the five-step p-value process, and compare p with α.
The p-value is the probability, assuming H0 is true, of a test statistic equal to or more extreme than observed. The deck also calls it the observed level of significance and thesmallest α at which H0 could be rejected. Source workflow: H0/H1 → choose α and n → select statistic/distribution → collect data and compute statistic+p → decide andconclude in context.
9.6 Two-Tail Z and t Tests ↔ Confidence Intervals
Use the same-level CI as a decision cross-check for two-tail Z and Student-t tests.
At the same α/confidence pairing, a two-tail Z or t test rejects H0 when μ0 is outside the corresponding (1−α) confidence interval and does not reject when μ0 lies inside,provided the same model/conditions are used.
9.7 Mean Test — σ Unknown: Student t
Unknown σ selects Student t with df=n−1; normality evidence is checked explicitly.
Use T=(X̄ −μ0)/(s/√n), df=n−1. The deck notes that σ is rarely known in real business settings. For the t route, compare sample statistics with normal properties, inspecthistogram/stem-and-leaf/boxplot symmetry, and inspect approximate linearity of the normal probability plot; then use T.INV/T.DIST/T.DIST.2T as appropriate.
9.8 One-Tail Mean Tests
Direction lives in H1; SDS188 uses equality in H0 for the one-tail teaching convention.
Lower: H0:μ=μ0, H1:μ<μ0. Upper: H0:μ=μ0, H1:μ>μ0. Put all α in the directed tail and use the matching Z/t probability.
9.9 One-Proportion Z Tests
Binary categorical data → p → conditions → Z → decision.
p=x/n. Check nπ0≥5 and n(1−π0)≥5. Then use Z=(p−π0)/√[π0(1−π0)/n]. Since x=np, the deck also gives the equivalent count form Z=(x−nπ0)/√[nπ0(1−π0)]. Use the critical-
value or p-value approach.
9.10 Power, Planning and Statistical Responsibility
Practical meaning, ethical planning, complete β/power computation and sensitivity to α/effect size.
Large n can make tiny effects statistically significant; small n can miss important effects. Plan the goal, hypotheses, tail, random sample, data type, α, sample size/power,procedure and contextual conclusion. Ethical issues named by the deck include human subjects, data collection, test choice, significance level, cleaning/discarding data andfailure to report pertinent findings. Tutorial 9 Q21 is covered at α=.05 and α=.01; Q22 supplies the tyre-life power application.

8.1 · Point Estimates, Interval Estimates & Good Estimators
Objective: Distinguish a population parameter from its sample estimator, distinguish point from interval estimation, and explain unbiasedness, consistency and relative efficiency in the terminology usedby the Chapter 8 slides.
Recognise
The question asks what one sample statistic estimates.The question contrasts a single estimate with a range.The question asks which estimator is unbiased, consistent or relatively more efficient.
Explanation
A population parameter is an unknown population characteristic such as the mean μ or proportion π. A sample statistic such as X̄  or p is used to estimate it.
A point estimate is one number. A confidence interval adds information about precision by giving lower and upper confidence limits around a point estimate.
An unbiased estimator has expected value equal to the population parameter. A consistent estimator gets closer to the parameter as sample size increases. Among two unbiased estimators, the one withsmaller variance is relatively more efficient.
Formula / decision object: Population mean μ ← estimated by X̄ ; population proportion π ← estimated by p. Point estimate = one statistic; interval estimate = point estimate plus quantifieduncertainty.
Excel route
1. No special Excel function is needed for these definitions. In workbook questions, identify the statistic you are calculating before building any formula.2. If raw values are supplied and the point estimate is a mean, AVERAGE(range) produces X̄ ; if the point estimate is a proportion, compute successes / n.
Say aloud: “The parameter belongs to the population; the estimator comes from the sample. A point estimate is one number. A confidence interval adds uncertainty. Unbiased means centred correctly,consistent means improving with larger n, and relative efficiency means smaller variance among unbiased estimators.”
Worked example · Source estimation-process diagram
1. The population mean μ is unknown.2. A random sample is drawn from the population.3. The sample mean X̄ =50 is the point estimate of μ.4. An interval such as 40 to 60 adds a quantified range around the estimate.
Result: X̄  is the point estimator; the confidence interval is the interval estimate for μ.
Verify: Do not describe a confidence interval as an interval for individual observations.
Common trap: Do not swap the population parameter with the sample statistic, and do not treat a confidence interval for a parameter as a range for individual observations.
Exit criterion: You can name the parameter, its estimator, point versus interval estimation, and all three estimator characteristics without looking.

8.2 · Confidence Level, α, Margin of Error & Correct Interpretation
Objective: Understand what a confidence interval says, connect confidence level to α and α/2, identify margin of error, and avoid the fixed-interval probability mistake.
Recognise
The question asks what “95% confidence” means.The question asks for α or α/2 from a confidence level.The question asks what widens or narrows an interval, or what the margin of error represents.
Explanation
The Chapter 8 source gives the general structure: point estimate ± (critical value)(standard error). The amount added and subtracted is the margin of error.
For a 95% confidence level, 1−α=0.95, so α=0.05 and α/2=0.025 in each tail for a two-sided interval.
The long-run interpretation is procedural: 95% of intervals constructed in this way would contain the unknown parameter. Once one particular interval has been calculated, the fixed parameter either liesin it or it does not.
Formula / decision object: Confidence interval = Point estimate ± Margin of error, where Margin of error = Critical value × Standard error.
Excel route
1. Excel may calculate the critical value and standard error, but you must still identify the confidence coefficient, α and whether the interval is two-sided.2. For a 95% two-sided normal interval, the source uses zα/2=1.96.
Say aloud: “Confidence describes the long-run interval procedure, not a probability that the fixed parameter moves around. For 95%, alpha is 0.05 and each tail gets 0.025.”
Worked example · Cereal-fill confidence interpretation
1. The source considers repeated samples of the same size.2. Each sample gives a different X̄  and therefore a different interval.3. Most intervals produced by the 95% procedure contain μ; some do not.4. In practice only one sample is observed, so we report that we are 95% confident that its interval contains μ.
Result: Confidence is a property of the repeated procedure used to construct the interval.
Verify: Never reinterpret a confidence interval as the range containing 95% of individual data values.
Common trap: Do not say there is a 95% probability that the fixed parameter lies in the one interval already computed, and do not say 95% of individuals lie inside the CI.
Exit criterion: You can move from confidence level → α → α/2 and can give the correct long-run interpretation aloud.

8.3 · Confidence Interval for μ when Population σ is Known
Objective: Recognise the known-σ mean interval, check the source assumptions, calculate zα/2, standard error, margin of error and limits, then interpret the interval in context.
Recognise
The target parameter is the population mean μ.The population standard deviation σ is explicitly known.The population is normal, or the source rule permits a large sample when it is not normal.
Explanation
When σ is known, the source uses the standard normal critical value zα/2 and standard error σ/√n.
The source states the assumptions as: σ known; population normally distributed; if the population is not normal, use a large sample n>30.
Higher confidence uses a larger critical value and therefore a wider interval. Larger n reduces standard error and narrows the interval.
Formula / decision object: X̄  ± zα/2(σ/√n)
Excel route
1. Build the standard error as =sigma/SQRT(n).2. For a two-sided confidence level stored as CL, obtain a positive critical value with =NORM.S.INV(1-(1-CL)/2) or use the equivalent source-style absolute value of NORM.S.INV((1-CL)/2).3. Lower = Xbar − z*SE; Upper = Xbar + z*SE.
Say aloud: “Mean parameter, sigma known: use Z. Standard error is sigma over root n. Interval is X-bar plus or minus z alpha over two times that standard error.”
Worked example · Tutorial 8 Q1: X̄ =85, σ=8, n=64, 95% confidence
1. Target: μ. Population σ is known, so select the Z interval.2. 95% confidence gives zα/2=1.96.3. SE=σ/√n=8/√64=1.4. Margin=1.96×1=1.96.5. Limits: 85−1.96=83.04 and 85+1.96=86.96.
Result: 95% CI: (83.04, 86.96).
Verify: The interval is centred at X̄ =85 and is symmetric because the same margin is added and subtracted.
Common trap: Do not use Student t merely because a sample is involved: when population sigma is explicitly known, the source route is Z. Also do not forget the square root of n in the standard
error.
Exit criterion: You can recognise known σ instantly and calculate the complete interval from confidence level to interpretation without prompts.

8.4 · Student t Distribution, Degrees of Freedom & the Supplied t Table
Objective: Explain why Student t replaces Z when σ is unknown, calculate df=n−1, understand the shape of the t family, and read the exact supplied t table correctly.
Recognise
Population σ is unknown and sample s is used instead.A critical t value is requested from confidence level and n.The question gives a t table and asks you to locate a row/column.
Explanation
Replacing unknown σ with sample s introduces extra uncertainty because s varies from sample to sample. The source therefore uses Student t.

8.9 · Finite Population Correction for Confidence Intervals
Objective: Recognise when sampling without replacement from finite N requires the course FPC rule, understand why the correction reduces standard error, and apply it to mean/proportion intervals.

Explanation

7.3 · Standard Error & Why Larger n Narrows the Distribution
Objective: Treat standard error as the standard deviation of a statistic across possible samples and explain exactly how n changes it.
Recognise
The question compares variability of sample means from different n.The question asks for the standard error of X̄ .The question asks why increasing sample size makes estimates more stable.
Explanation
Different samples produce different X̄  values. The standard error measures the spread of those possible means.
For the ordinary sample-mean model (sampling with replacement, or without replacement from an effectively infinite population), σ_X̄ =σ/√n. The square root matters: quadrupling n halves the standarderror. If sampling is without replacement from a finite population, the FPC in Lesson 7.12 can reduce this spread further.
Increasing n does not shift the centre μ_X̄ =μ. It narrows the distribution around the same target.
Formula / decision object: SE(X̄ )=σ/√n. If n is multiplied by k, SE is divided by √k.

For a sample mean X̄ , use centre μ and spread σ/√n. This distinction is one of the most important Chapter 7 exam traps.
If variance is supplied, convert it to standard deviation before using either model: σ=√variance.
Formula / decision object: One X: Z=(X−μ)/σ. Sample mean X̄ : Z=(X̄ −μ)/(σ/√n).
Excel route
1. One normal observation: =NORM.DIST(x,mu,sigma,TRUE).2. Sample mean: =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE).
Say aloud: “I name the random object first. One observation uses sigma. An average uses sigma over square-root n.”
Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q2 — one mark versus class average
1. Marks are normal with μ=78 and variance 36, so σ=6.2. Part (a) asks for one student mark below 75: z=(75−78)/6=−0.5, probability≈0.3085.3. Part (b) asks for the mean of n=50 students below 75: SE=6/√50≈0.8485.4. z=(75−78)/0.8485≈−3.5355, probability≈0.0002.
Result: The same boundary 75 has radically different probability because X and X̄  have different spreads.
Verify: The population did not change; the random object changed.
Common trap: Do not use σ for a sample mean and do not use σ/√n for a single observation.
Exit criterion: You can classify X versus X̄  before doing any arithmetic and interpret the result in terms of the correct object.

Objective: Solve forward probabilities for X̄  when population σ is known, with the tail/region matching the words.
Recognise

7.6 · Inverse Sample-Mean Boundaries & Central Symmetric Limits
Objective: Work backwards from a probability to the X̄  boundary and construct symmetric probability limits around μ.
Recognise

Explanation
Inverse problems reverse the ordinary workflow: probability → z → original X̄  scale.

For a central C interval, split α=1−C equally between the two tails and transform both z boundaries back with μ+z·SE.
Formula / decision object: Boundary = μ + z·σ/√n. Central limits: μ ± z*·σ/√n, where z*=Φ⁻¹((1+C)/2).
Excel route

Worked example · Tutorial 7 Q1(d)

Verify: Using 0.65 as the left area would put the boundary above μ and reverse the requested region.

7.7 · Central Limit Theorem: What Becomes Normal & When
Objective: Use the CLT correctly for non-normal populations while preserving the sampling-distribution centre and SE.
Recognise
The population is not stated to be normal or is explicitly skewed/exponential.The task asks about an average of many observations.You must decide whether the sampling distribution of X̄  is approximately normal.
Explanation
The CLT concerns the sampling distribution of X̄ . It does not transform the original population or individual observations into normal variables.
The course guideline says normal population → exact normal X̄  for any valid n; fairly symmetric populations may be adequate around n>15; most distributions around n>30. These are course rules ofthumb, not magical theorem cutoffs.
Under the CLT, the sampling distribution remains centred at μ with SE σ/√n. Only the approximate shape changes toward normal.
Formula / decision object: For sufficiently large n: X̄  ≈ N(μ,σ²/n), even when the original population is not normal.
Excel route
1. Once the CLT justifies the normal sampling model, use =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE) for cumulative-left sample-mean probabilities.2. Do not use an exponential CDF on the sample mean merely because each individual observation is exponential.
Say aloud: “The CLT makes the sampling distribution of the mean approximately normal. It does not make the population normal. The centre stays μ and the SE stays σ over square-root n.”
Worked example · Chapter 7 restaurant waiting-time example
1. Individual wait times have μ=8 min and σ=3 min; their population shape need not be normal.2. n=36, so the course CLT guideline supports an approximately normal X̄  distribution.3. SE=3/√36=0.5.4. P(7.8<X̄ <8.2): zL=−0.4, zU=0.4.5. Φ(0.4)−Φ(−0.4)=0.6554−0.3446=0.3108.

Verify: The statement is about sample means, not individual waiting times.
Common trap: Do not say “n=30 makes the population normal.” Also do not forget to convert the exponential rate to consistent time units before finding μ and σ.
