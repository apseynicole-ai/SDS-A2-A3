P50: 6.1 - Continuous Probability Foundations
P51: Explanation
P52: A discrete variable jumps between countable outcomes. A continuous variable can take any real value in an interval, so measurements such as time, length, height and volume are continuous.
P53: For a continuous distribution the vertical axis is density f(x), not probability. Probability is the area under the density curve over an interval. The full area is 1.
P54: A single point has zero width, so it has zero area. Therefore P(X=c)=0. That is also why P(X<c)=P(X≤c) for continuous X.
P55: Worked example - Why < and ≤ give the same continuous probability
P56: 1. Suppose we want P(X≤5).
P57: 2. Split it as P(X<5)+P(X=5).
P58: 3. For continuous X, P(X=5)=0.
P59: 4. Therefore P(X≤5)=P(X<5).
P60: Result: Including or excluding one boundary point does not change a continuous probability.
P61: Verify: If your reasoning depends on a positive probability at exactly one continuous value, stop and repair that idea.

P68: CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.2 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P69: 6.2 - Normal Distribution
P70: Explanation
P71: The normal distribution is continuous, symmetric and bell-shaped. Its centre is μ. In a perfectly normal model, mean = median = mode.
P72: In X~N(μ,σ²), the second parameter is variance. If you are given σ², take its square root before using the z-score formula.
P73: Changing μ shifts the curve left or right without changing its basic shape. Increasing σ spreads the same total area over a wider range, so the curve becomes wider and flatter.
P74: Any normal X can be translated to the standard normal Z~N(0,1) with z=(x-μ)/σ. A z-score tells you how many standard deviations x lies above or below μ.
P75: Worked example - Standardise a value
P76: 1. Given X~N(100,50²) and x=200.
P77: 2. Identify μ=100 and σ=50.
P78: 3. Compute z=(200-100)/50.
P79: 4. z=2.
P80: Result: x=200 is 2 standard deviations above the mean.
P81: Verify: A value above μ must produce a positive z-score; below μ must produce a negative one.

P89: 6.3 - Normal Regions & Forward Probability
P90: Explanation
P91: Do not begin with calculator buttons. First draw or identify the region in X-units. The wording determines the region.
P92: Below / less than means the left cumulative area. Above / greater than means a right tail, so use 1-left area. Between means upper left-area minus lower left-area.
P93: Then standardise any X boundary using z=(x-μ)/σ. The course Excel routes are NORM.DIST(x,μ,σ,TRUE) for X directly and NORM.S.DIST(z,TRUE) for Z.
P94: Worked example - Official download-time example: P(X<18.6)
P95: 1. Given X~N(18,5²). We want the area left of 18.6.
P96: 2. Standardise: z=(18.6-18)/5=0.12.
P97: 3. Find Φ(0.12)=0.5478.
P98: 4. Interpret: about 54.78% of download times are below 18.6 seconds.
P99: Result: P(X<18.6)=0.5478.
P100: Verify: 18.6 is slightly above the mean, so a left-tail probability slightly above 0.5 is sensible.

P108: 6.4 - Inverse Normal & Percentiles
P109: Explanation
P110: Forward normal problems give x and ask for probability. Inverse normal problems give probability or percentile and ask for x.
P111: The inverse tool expects cumulative area to the left. If the wording gives a right-tail percentage, convert it first. Example: top 10% means 10% to the right, so 90% to the left.
P112: Find z from the cumulative-left probability, then return to original units with x=μ+zσ. Excel can do this directly with NORM.INV(prob,μ,σ), or use NORM.S.INV(prob) then back-transform.
P113: Worked example - 20th percentile of N(18,5²)
P114: 1. We need x with P(X<x)=0.20.
P115: 2. Inverse standard normal gives z≈-0.8416.
P116: 3. Back-transform: x=18+(-0.8416)(5).
P117: 4. x≈13.792 seconds.
P118: Result: The 20th percentile is about 13.8 seconds.
P119: Verify: A 20th percentile must lie below the mean, so a negative z and x<18 are sensible.

P127: 6.5 - Evaluation of Normality
P128: Explanation
P129: Real data are rarely perfectly normal. The task is usually to decide whether a normal approximation is reasonable.
P130: Numerical evidence includes mean, median and mode for symmetry and the empirical 68-95-99.7 rule. Mean greater than median suggests right skew; mean less than median suggests left skew.
P131: Graphical evidence includes histograms, frequency polygons, stem-and-leaf displays, boxplots and the normal probability plot. A normal probability plot should be approximately linear.
P132: The strongest conclusion uses several indicators together. A bell-shaped histogram alone does not prove normality, and mean≈median alone does not prove normality.
P133: Worked example - Small-sample empirical-rule evidence
P134: 1. A worked example has n=15.
P135: 2. Observed within 1s: 73% versus theoretical 68%.
P136: 3. Observed within 2s: 100% versus theoretical 95%.
P137: 4. Observed within 3s: 100% versus theoretical 99.7%.
P138: 5. For a small sample, these are treated as reasonably close; combine this with symmetry and plot evidence before concluding.
P139: Result: The example is classified as approximately normal based on the collective evidence.
P140: Verify: Do not write "normal because 73%≈68%". Your conclusion should explicitly combine the available evidence.

P167: CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.7 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P168: 6.7 - Uniform Distribution
P169: Explanation
P170: A uniform distribution is continuous and rectangular. Every equal-width interval inside [a,b] has the same probability because the density height is constant.
P171: The height is f(x)=1/(b-a). The height itself is not a probability; probability is rectangle area = width×height.
P172: For c<d inside [a,b], P(c<X<d)=(d-c)/(b-a). The mean is (a+b)/2 and the standard deviation is sqrt((b-a)^2/12).
P173: Worked example - Tutorial 6 Q8: Uniform(0,10), P(5<X<7)
P174: 1. Identify a=0, b=10.
P175: 2. Total width is 10. Target interval width is 7-5=2.
P176: 3. Probability=2/10=0.20.
P177: 4. Equivalent area method: height=1/10=0.1, area=2×0.1=0.20.
P178: Result: P(5<X<7)=0.20.
P179: Verify: The interval uses 20% of the total horizontal width, so 0.20 is visually sensible.

P186: CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.8 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P187: 6.8 - Exponential Distribution
P188: Explanation
P189: The exponential distribution models continuous waiting time between events. It is right-skewed and starts at 0.
P190: λ is a rate per unit time or opportunity, not the waiting time itself. Mean waiting time is 1/λ and variance is 1/λ².
P191: Use P(X≤x)=1-e^(-λx) for a left tail and P(X≥x)=e^(-λx) for a right tail. For between events subtract two cumulative left areas.
P192: Poisson and exponential share the same rate context: Poisson counts how many events occur in a fixed interval; exponential measures the time until/between events.
P193: Before calculating, λ and x must use the same time unit.
P194: Worked example - Toll plaza: 50 cars/min, next car within 3 seconds
P195: 1. Random object: waiting time, so use exponential.
P196: 2. λ=50 per minute. Convert 3 seconds to 0.05 minute.
P197: 3. P(X≤0.05)=1-e^(-50×0.05).
P198: 4. =1-e^(-2.5)≈0.9179.
P199: Result: Probability ≈0.9179.
P200: Verify: With 50 arrivals per minute, a very short wait is quite likely, so a large probability makes sense.
P201: Sources: 2026 Chapter 6 notes §§3.1-3.1.5 | Exponential probabilities worked sheet | Tutorial 6 + solutions Q10-Q13 | Chapter 6 lecturer transcript toll-plaza example

P207: CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.9 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P208: 6.9 - Inverse Exponential
P209: Explanation
P210: Inverse exponential asks for time x rather than probability. Start from the exponential probability equation and solve for x using natural logarithms.
P211: If P(X≤x)=α, then 1-e^(-λx)=α, so e^(-λx)=1-α and x=-ln(1-α)/λ.
P212: If the question gives a right-tail probability q=P(X>x), it is even quicker: e^(-λx)=q, so x=-ln(q)/λ.
P213: This is not NORM.INV. Exponential is asymmetric and uses ln, not z-scores.
P214: Worked example - 80th percentile when λ=2 per week
P215: 1. Set P(X<x)=0.80.
P216: 2. 1-e^(-2x)=0.80, so e^(-2x)=0.20.
P217: 3. Take ln: -2x=ln(0.20).
P218: 4. x=-ln(0.20)/2≈0.8047 weeks.
P219: Result: P80≈0.8047 weeks.
P220: Verify: An 80th percentile is a positive waiting time. Substituting it back should give cumulative probability near 0.80.

P227: CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.10 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P228: 6.10 - Normal Approximation to the Binomial
P229: Explanation
P230: The binomial distribution is discrete, but for sufficiently large expected success and failure counts its shape can be approximated by a normal curve.
P231: Current Chapter 6 conditions are nπ>5 and n(1-π)>5. If either fails, do not use this approximation.
P232: The approximating normal model has μ=nπ and σ=sqrt(nπ(1-π)).
P233: Because the binomial lives on integer points while the normal curve is continuous, we use a ±0.5 continuity correction to preserve the area around each integer.
P234: Exact x becomes x-0.5<Xa<x+0.5. X≤x becomes Xa<x+0.5. X≥x becomes Xa>x-0.5. Strict inequalities first need translation to the adjacent inclusive integer event.
P235: Worked example - Tutorial 6 Q14: n=100, π=0.20, approximate P(X=25)
P236: 1. Check: nπ=20>5 and n(1-π)=80>5.
P237: 2. Approximate model: μ=20, σ=sqrt(16)=4.
P238: 3. Continuity correction: P(X=25)≈P(24.5<Xa<25.5).
P239: 4. zL=(24.5-20)/4=1.125≈1.13; zU=(25.5-20)/4=1.375≈1.38.
P240: 5. Probability≈Φ(1.38)-Φ(1.13)=0.9162-0.8708=0.0454.
P241: Result: P(X=25)≈0.0454.
P242: Verify: Without the ±0.5 slice, a continuous model would assign zero probability to exactly 25.

P253: CHAPTER 7 | LESSON 7.1 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P254: 7.1 - Population → Sample → Statistic → Sampling Distribution
P255: Recognise it
P256: The question asks what a sampling distribution is.
P257: The question contrasts a population, one sample, one statistic and repeated possible statistics.
P258: The wording asks why two random samples can produce different means or proportions.
P259: Explanation
P260: A population is the full collection of units. A sample is one subset drawn from it. A parameter such as μ, σ or π belongs to the population; a statistic such as X̄ or p is calculated from a sample.
P261: Different random samples usually contain different observations, so the statistic changes from sample to sample. The sampling distribution describes that variability mathematically.
P262: The Chapter 7 objective is not merely to calculate one sample statistic; it is to reason about the distribution of the statistic before the sample is observed.
P263: Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
P264: 1. Excel can calculate a realised sample statistic, but it does not decide what the random object is for you.
P265: 2. Before typing a function, name the statistic whose repeated values form the sampling distribution.
P266: Worked example - Chapter 7 GPA thought experiment
P267: 1. Imagine repeatedly selecting 50 students from the same college.
P268: 2. Each sample produces a possibly different sample mean GPA.
P269: 3. Collect the possible sample means rather than the 50 raw GPAs from one sample.
P270: 4. The distribution of those possible means is the sampling distribution of X̄.
P271: Result: The random object is X̄, and the sampling distribution is the probability model for its possible values.
P272: Verify: Do not call one realised set of 50 GPAs a sampling distribution.

P280: 7.2 - Exact Sampling Distributions & Unbiasedness
P281: Recognise it
P282: A tiny finite population is listed explicitly.
P283: The task says "all possible samples" or asks you to construct the sampling distribution exactly.
P284: The question asks why the sample mean is unbiased.
P285: Explanation
P286: The source builds a toy population 18,20,22,24 and lists every ordered sample of size 2 with replacement. Each possible sample has its own X̄.
P287: An exact sampling distribution combines repeated statistic values with their probabilities. Its mean is the expected value of the statistic.
P288: When E(X̄)=μ, X̄ is an unbiased estimator of μ. Unbiased does not mean every sample mean equals μ.
P289: Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
P290: 1. For a small enumeration, Excel can list sample combinations and use AVERAGE for each sample, but the key idea is the complete set of possible statistic values.
P291: 2. For Tutorial 7 Q4, sampling is without replacement, so use combinations rather than duplicating ordered draws as if replacement were allowed.
P292: Worked example - Source toy population 18,20,22,24
P293: 1. Population mean μ=(18+20+22+24)/4=21.
P294: 2. With replacement and n=2 there are 4²=16 ordered samples.
P295: 3. Their sample means range from 18 to 24 and are no longer uniformly distributed.
P296: 4. The mean of the 16 sample means is 21.
P297: Result: μ_X̄=21=μ, so X̄ is unbiased for μ.
P298: Verify: The sampling distribution can have a different shape and smaller spread than the population while keeping the same centre.

P306: 7.3 - Standard Error & Why Larger n Narrows the Distribution
P307: Recognise it
P308: The question compares variability of sample means from different n.
P309: The question asks for the standard error of X̄.
P310: The question asks why increasing sample size makes estimates more stable.
P311: Explanation
P312: Different samples produce different X̄ values. The standard error measures the spread of those possible means.
P313: For the ordinary sample-mean model (sampling with replacement, or without replacement from an effectively infinite population), σ_X̄=σ/√n. The square root matters: quadrupling n halves the standard error. If sampling is without replacement from a finite population, the FPC in Lesson 7.12 can reduce this spread further.
P314: Increasing n does not shift the centre μ_X̄=μ. It narrows the distribution around the same target.
P315: Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
P316: 1. In Excel use =sigma/SQRT(n) when population σ is known.
P317: 2. Do not use =sigma/n. SQRT(n) is the entire reason the gain from sample size has diminishing returns.
P318: Worked example - Tutorial 7 Q4 comparison
P319: 1. For the population 1,3,6,7,9,10, all without-replacement n=2 sample means have mean 6.
P320: 2. Their exact sampling-distribution SD is 2.0000.
P321: 3. For all without-replacement n=3 sample means, the centre is still 6.
P322: 4. Their exact sampling-distribution SD is about 1.4142.
P323: Result: The n=3 sampling distribution has less variability while retaining centre 6.
P324: Verify: This exact Tutorial 7 Q4 enumeration is without replacement from N=6, so its SDs (2.0000 and 1.4142) already reflect finite-population reduction; they should not be mistaken for the ordinary σ/√n values. The key comparison is still that n=3 varies less than n=2.
P325: Sources: Chapter 7 slides pp. 9-11 and 15 | Tutorial 7 Q4(c) and Q11

P332: 7.4 - One Observation X versus Sample Mean X̄
P333: Recognise it
P334: The wording says one person/item/time/mark versus the average of n observations.
P335: A variance may be given and must first be converted to σ.
P336: The task asks you to interpret a probability about X̄ in context.
P337: Explanation
P338: For one observation X from a normal population, use centre μ and spread σ.
P339: For a sample mean X̄, use centre μ and spread σ/√n. This distinction is one of the most important Chapter 7 exam traps.
P340: If variance is supplied, convert it to standard deviation before using either model: σ=√variance.
P341: Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
P342: 1. One normal observation: =NORM.DIST(x,mu,sigma,TRUE).
P343: 2. Sample mean: =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE).
P344: Worked example - Tutorial 7 Q2 - one mark versus class average
P345: 1. Marks are normal with μ=78 and variance 36, so σ=6.
P346: 2. Part (a) asks for one student mark below 75: z=(75-78)/6=-0.5, probability≈0.3085.
P347: 3. Part (b) asks for the mean of n=50 students below 75: SE=6/√50≈0.8485.
P348: 4. z=(75-78)/0.8485≈-3.5355, probability≈0.0002.
P349: Result: The same boundary 75 has radically different probability because X and X̄ have different spreads.
P350: Verify: The population did not change; the random object changed.

P358: 7.5 - Sample-Mean Probabilities with Known σ: Left, Between & Right
P359: Recognise it
P360: A normal population or justified normal sampling model is available.
P361: The random object is X̄ and population σ is known.
P362: The task asks below, between or above a stated sample-mean boundary.
P363: Explanation
P364: For a normal population, the sampling distribution of X̄ is exactly normal for every valid n, with centre μ and SE σ/√n.
P365: Standardise the sample-mean boundary using the SE, not σ.
P366: Excel cumulative functions return left area. "Above" therefore requires a complement unless you use an equivalent direct route.
P367: Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
P368: 1. Direct left: =NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE).
P369: 2. Right: =1-NORM.DIST(xbar,mu,sigma/SQRT(n),TRUE). Between: upper cumulative minus lower cumulative.
P370: Worked example - Tutorial 7 Q1(a-c)
P371: 1. μ=100, σ=10, n=25, so SE=10/5=2.
P372: 2. (a) P(X̄<95): z=-2.5, probability≈0.0062.
P373: 3. (b) P(95<X̄<97.5): zL=-2.5, zU=-1.25, probability≈0.0994.
P374: 4. (c) P(X̄>102.2): z=1.1, probability≈0.1357.

P601: 8.1 - Point Estimates, Interval Estimates & Good Estimators
P602: Recognise it
P603: The question asks what one sample statistic estimates.
P604: The question contrasts a single estimate with a range.
P605: The question asks which estimator is unbiased, consistent or relatively more efficient.
P606: Explanation
P607: A population parameter is an unknown population characteristic such as the mean μ or proportion π. A sample statistic such as X̄ or p is used to estimate it.
P608: A point estimate is one number. A confidence interval adds information about precision by giving lower and upper confidence limits around a point estimate.
P609: An unbiased estimator has expected value equal to the population parameter. A consistent estimator gets closer to the parameter as sample size increases. Among two unbiased estimators, the one with smaller variance is relatively more efficient.

P612: 2. If raw values are supplied and the point estimate is a mean, AVERAGE(range) produces X̄; if the point estimate is a proportion, compute successes / n.

P615: 2. A random sample is drawn from the population.
P616: 3. The sample mean X̄=50 is the point estimate of μ.
P617: 4. An interval such as 40 to 60 adds a quantified range around the estimate.
P618: Result: X̄ is the point estimator; the confidence interval is the interval estimate for μ.
P619: Verify: Do not describe a confidence interval as an interval for individual observations.

P821: 8.9 - Finite Population Correction for Confidence Intervals
P822: Recognise it
P823: Sampling is without replacement from a finite population of size N.
P824: The sampling fraction n/N must be checked.
P825: The source uses the FPC when n/N>0.05; below 5% its practical effect is little or none.
P826: Explanation
P827: The finite-population source says use the correction factor when more than 5% of the population is sampled without replacement.
P828: The factor √[(N-n)/(N-1)] multiplies the usual standard error. Because it is at most 1, it reduces the standard error and therefore narrows the confidence interval.
P829: The same correction multiplies the mean standard error s/√n and the proportion standard error √[p(1-p)/n].
P830: Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
P831: 1. FPC: =SQRT((N-n)/(N-1)).
P832: 2. Multiply the ordinary SE by the FPC before multiplying by the critical value.
P833: 3. Do not apply the correction merely because N is given; first check sampling without replacement and n/N.
P834: Worked example - Finite-population source Example 8.10: N=200, n=27, X̄=45.2222, s=23.1472, 95%
P835: 1. Sampling is without replacement and n/N=27/200=.135>0.05, so FPC matters.
P836: 2. df=26 and the source uses t=2.0555.
P837: 3. FPC=√[(200-27)/(200-1)].
P838: 4. Apply t×(s/√n)×FPC as the margin.
P839: 5. The source obtains limits 36.6846 and 53.7598.
P840: Result: 95% CI: (36.6846, 53.7598).
P841: Verify: Because FPC<1, the interval is narrower than the corresponding no-FPC interval.
P842: Sources: Chapter 8 slides pp. 59-63 | Dedicated finite-population extract §8.7 pp. 1-2 | Tutorial 8 Q11-Q12

P850: Recognise it

P876: 8.11 - Bootstrapping: Why, Resampling Logic & Percentile Confidence Limits
P877: Recognise it
P878: The population cannot reasonably be assumed normal.
P879: You have an observed sample and can resample from it with replacement.
P880: The task asks for a bootstrap confidence interval for a population mean.
P881: Explanation
P882: The supplied bootstrapping source says that when one cannot assume a normal population, bootstrap methods use repeated resampling with replacement from the initial sample and make no assumption about the underlying population distribution.

P969: 9.1 - Hypothesis Foundations
P970: Explanation
P971: A hypothesis is a claim about a population parameter such as the population mean μ or population proportion π. The sample statistic is evidence; it is not what H0 is written about.
P972: H0 represents the claim/status quo to be validated and carries equality in the SDS188 treatment. H1 is mutually exclusive and challenges H0.

P978: 3. Write H0: μ=500.
P979: 4. If the research question asks whether the mean differs, write H1: μ≠500.
P980: 5. Begin analysis under the assumption H0 is true.
P981: Result: H0: μ=500; H1: μ≠500.
P982: Verify: Both statements concern μ and are mutually exclusive.

P989: CHAPTER 9 | LESSON 9.2 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
P990: 9.2 - Decision Risk: α, β & Power
P991: Explanation
P992: Type I error means rejecting H0 when H0 is true. Its probability is α, the significance level.
P993: Type II error means not rejecting H0 when H0 is false. Its probability is β. Power is 1-β: the probability of rejecting a false H0.
P994: The confidence coefficient is 1-α. Type I and Type II errors cannot occur in the same test outcome because H0 cannot simultaneously be true and false.
P995: For fixed n, reducing α makes rejection harder and β increases. Power rises when the true parameter is farther from the null value or n increases; larger variance makes detection harder and increases β.
P996: Worked example - Why distance from μ0 changes power
P997: 1. Suppose H0: μ=100.
P998: 2. A true μ=90 is 10 units from the null.
P999: 3. A true μ=75 is 25 units from the null.
P1000: 4. The farther alternative creates more separation between the sampling distributions.
P1001: 5. Therefore β is smaller and power larger at μ=75 than at μ=90.
P1002: Result: Closer alternatives are harder to detect; farther alternatives have higher power.
P1003: Verify: This is exactly the logic behind Tutorial 9 Q3.
P1004: Sources: Official Chapter 9 deck: Type I/II error and power sections | SDS Tutorial 9 Q1-3, Q21-22
