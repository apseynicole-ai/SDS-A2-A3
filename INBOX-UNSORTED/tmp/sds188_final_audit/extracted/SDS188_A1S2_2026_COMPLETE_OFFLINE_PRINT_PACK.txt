6.1 - Continuous Probability Foundations
OBJECTIVE
Distinguish discrete from continuous random variables and explain why probability for a continuous variable is area, not the 
height of the density curve.
Explanation
A discrete variable jumps between countable outcomes. A continuous variable can take any real value in an interval, so 
measurements such as time, length, height and volume are continuous.
For a continuous distribution the vertical axis is density f(x), not probability. Probability is the area under the density curve over 
an interval. The full area is 1.
A single point has zero width, so it has zero area. Therefore P(X=c)=0. That is also why P(X<c)=P(X≤c) for continuous X.
SAY ALOUD
Discrete means countable points. Continuous means any measured value in a range. For continuous variables, probability is 
area under the density curve, so one exact point has probability zero.
Worked example - Why < and ≤ give the same continuous probability
1. Suppose we want P(X≤5).
2. Split it as P(X<5)+P(X=5).
3. For continuous X, P(X=5)=0.
4. Therefore P(X≤5)=P(X<5).
Result: Including or excluding one boundary point does not change a continuous probability.
Verify: If your reasoning depends on a positive probability at exactly one continuous value, stop and repair that idea.
KNOWLEDGE CHECK - answer before looking at the key

A. P(X=4)=0
B. P(X=4)=f(4)
C. P(X=4) must be positive
YOUR TURN - work independently

B. P(X=8)

CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.2 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
6.2 - Normal Distribution
OBJECTIVE
Read normal notation, predict how μ and σ change the curve, recognise the standard normal distribution, and standardise X 
to Z.
Explanation
The normal distribution is continuous, symmetric and bell-shaped. Its centre is μ. In a perfectly normal model, mean = median = 
mode.
In X~N(μ,σ²), the second parameter is variance. If you are given σ², take its square root before using the z-score formula.
Changing μ shifts the curve left or right without changing its basic shape. Increasing σ spreads the same total area over a wider 
range, so the curve becomes wider and flatter.
Any normal X can be translated to the standard normal Z~N(0,1) with z=(x-μ)/σ. A z-score tells you how many standard 
deviations x lies above or below μ.
SAY ALOUD
Mu sets the centre. Sigma sets the spread. Standardising subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, so z tells 
me how many standard deviations the value is from the mean.
Worked example - Standardise a value
1. Given X~N(100,50²) and x=200.
2. Identify μ=100 and σ=50.
3. Compute z=(200-100)/50.
4. z=2.
Result: x=200 is 2 standard deviations above the mean.
Verify: A value above μ must produce a positive z-score; below μ must produce a negative one.

6.3 - Normal Regions & Forward Probability
OBJECTIVE
Translate "below", "above" and "between" into the correct shaded region, standardise boundaries, and calculate the required 
normal area.
Explanation
Do not begin with calculator buttons. First draw or identify the region in X-units. The wording determines the region.
Below / less than means the left cumulative area. Above / greater than means a right tail, so use 1-left area. Between means 
upper left-area minus lower left-area.
Then standardise any X boundary using z=(x-μ)/σ. The course Excel routes are NORM.DIST(x,μ,σ,TRUE) for X directly and 
NORM.S.DIST(z,TRUE) for Z.
SAY ALOUD
Below is left. Above is one minus left. Between is upper left area minus lower left area. I draw the region first, then 
standardise, then calculate.
Worked example - Official download-time example: P(X<18.6)
1. Given X~N(18,5²). We want the area left of 18.6.
2. Standardise: z=(18.6-18)/5=0.12.
3. Find Φ(0.12)=0.5478.
4. Interpret: about 54.78% of download times are below 18.6 seconds.
Result: P(X<18.6)=0.5478.
Verify: 18.6 is slightly above the mean, so a left-tail probability slightly above 0.5 is sensible.

6.4 - Inverse Normal & Percentiles
OBJECTIVE
Recognise when probability is known but the boundary is unknown, convert wording to cumulative-left area, find z, and 
transform back to X.
Explanation
Forward normal problems give x and ask for probability. Inverse normal problems give probability or percentile and ask for x.
The inverse tool expects cumulative area to the left. If the wording gives a right-tail percentage, convert it first. Example: top 
10% means 10% to the right, so 90% to the left.
Find z from the cumulative-left probability, then return to original units with x=μ+zσ. Excel can do this directly with 
NORM.INV(prob,μ,σ), or use NORM.S.INV(prob) then back-transform.
SAY ALOUD
Inverse normal means the area is known and the boundary is unknown. I convert everything to left area, find z, then use x 
equals mu plus z sigma.
Worked example - 20th percentile of N(18,5²)
1. We need x with P(X<x)=0.20.
2. Inverse standard normal gives z≈-0.8416.
3. Back-transform: x=18+(-0.8416)(5).
4. x≈13.792 seconds.
Result: The 20th percentile is about 13.8 seconds.
Verify: A 20th percentile must lie below the mean, so a negative z and x<18 are sensible.

CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.5 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
6.5 - Evaluation of Normality
OBJECTIVE
Judge whether data are approximately normal by integrating descriptive, empirical-rule and graphical evidence instead of 
relying on one indicator.
Explanation
Real data are rarely perfectly normal. The task is usually to decide whether a normal approximation is reasonable.
Numerical evidence includes mean, median and mode for symmetry and the empirical 68-95-99.7 rule. Mean greater than 
median suggests right skew; mean less than median suggests left skew.
Graphical evidence includes histograms, frequency polygons, stem-and-leaf displays, boxplots and the normal probability plot. A 
normal probability plot should be approximately linear.
The strongest conclusion uses several indicators together. A bell-shaped histogram alone does not prove normality, and 
mean≈median alone does not prove normality.
SAY ALOUD
Normality is a collective judgement. I check centre and symmetry, empirical-rule proportions, and plots. No single check 
proves the model.
Worked example - Small-sample empirical-rule evidence
1. A worked example has n=15.
2. Observed within 1s: 73% versus theoretical 68%.
3. Observed within 2s: 100% versus theoretical 95%.
4. Observed within 3s: 100% versus theoretical 99.7%.
5. For a small sample, these are treated as reasonably close; combine this with symmetry and plot evidence before concluding.
Result: The example is classified as approximately normal based on the collective evidence.
Verify: Do not write "normal because 73%≈68%". Your conclusion should explicitly combine the available evidence.
KNOWLEDGE CHECK - answer before looking at the key

A. Use several numerical and graphical indicators together

6.6 - Excel Normality Laboratory
OBJECTIVE
Construct a normal probability plot correctly in Excel and combine it with descriptive/histogram evidence to reach a statistical 
conclusion.
Explanation
The normal probability plot is built from ordered observed values and the theoretical standard-normal quantiles they should 
correspond to if the data were normal.
The course workflow is: sort X ascending → create index j → calculate αj=j/(n+1) → convert αj to Z with NORM.S.INV → 
scatterplot Z on the x-axis and ordered X on the y-axis → judge linearity.
This is a separate evidence channel from statistical understanding. Knowing the Excel clicks is not the same as knowing why a 
straight line supports normality.
SAY ALOUD
Sort X, number the rows j, calculate j over n plus one, turn each probability into Z with NORM.S.INV, plot Z horizontally and X 
vertically, then judge whether the points are roughly linear.
Worked example - Supplied commuting-time workbook (n=15)
1. For j=1: α=1/16=0.0625.
2. NORM.S.INV(0.0625)≈-1.5341.
3. The first ordered X is 24.6. Plot the point (Z,X)=(-1.5341,24.6).
4. Repeat through j=15, ending at α=15/16=0.9375 and Z≈1.5341.
5. Use a scatterplot and assess whether the cloud follows an approximately straight line.
Result: The plot is a theoretical-quantile check: approximate linearity supports normality.
Verify: Axes matter: Z belongs on x, ordered X on y in the official workflow.

CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.7 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
6.7 - Uniform Distribution
OBJECTIVE
Recognise a uniform model, identify bounds a and b, use rectangle area for probabilities, and calculate the mean and spread.
Explanation
A uniform distribution is continuous and rectangular. Every equal-width interval inside [a,b] has the same probability because 
the density height is constant.
The height is f(x)=1/(b-a). The height itself is not a probability; probability is rectangle area = width×height.
For c<d inside [a,b], P(c<X<d)=(d-c)/(b-a). The mean is (a+b)/2 and the standard deviation is sqrt((b-a)^2/12).
SAY ALOUD
Uniform means a rectangle from a to b. Height is one over b minus a, but probability is area. Interval probability is interval 
width divided by total width.
Worked example - Tutorial 6 Q8: Uniform(0,10), P(5<X<7)
1. Identify a=0, b=10.
2. Total width is 10. Target interval width is 7-5=2.
3. Probability=2/10=0.20.
4. Equivalent area method: height=1/10=0.1, area=2×0.1=0.20.
Result: P(5<X<7)=0.20.
Verify: The interval uses 20% of the total horizontal width, so 0.20 is visually sensible.
KNOWLEDGE CHECK - answer before looking at the key

A. Density height
B. Every interval probability
C. The mean

6.8 - Exponential Distribution
OBJECTIVE
Recognise exponential waiting-time problems, interpret λ as a rate, align units, and solve left-, right- and between-probability 
questions.
Explanation
The exponential distribution models continuous waiting time between events. It is right-skewed and starts at 0.
λ is a rate per unit time or opportunity, not the waiting time itself. Mean waiting time is 1/λ and variance is 1/λ².
Use P(X≤x)=1-e^(-λx) for a left tail and P(X≥x)=e^(-λx) for a right tail. For between events subtract two cumulative left areas.
Poisson and exponential share the same rate context: Poisson counts how many events occur in a fixed interval; exponential 
measures the time until/between events.
Before calculating, λ and x must use the same time unit.
SAY ALOUD
Poisson counts events. Exponential waits for the next event. Lambda is a rate, and lambda times time must use matching 
units.
Worked example - Toll plaza: 50 cars/min, next car within 3 seconds
1. Random object: waiting time, so use exponential.
2. λ=50 per minute. Convert 3 seconds to 0.05 minute.
3. P(X≤0.05)=1-e^(-50×0.05).
4. =1-e^(-2.5)≈0.9179.
Result: Probability ≈0.9179.
Verify: With 50 arrivals per minute, a very short wait is quite likely, so a large probability makes sense.
KNOWLEDGE CHECK - answer before looking at the key

A. Exponential
B. Poisson
C. Uniform

CHAPTER 6 | LESSON 6.9 | OFFLINE GUIDED LEARN
6.9 - Inverse Exponential
OBJECTIVE
Solve for an unknown waiting-time boundary when a cumulative or tail probability is given, and keep it conceptually separate  
from inverse normal.
Explanation
Inverse exponential asks for time x rather than probability. Start from the exponential probability equation and solve for x using 
natural logarithms.
If P(X≤x)=α, then 1-e^(-λx)=α, so e^(-λx)=1-α and x=-ln(1-α)/λ.
If the question gives a right-tail probability q=P(X>x), it is even quicker: e^(-λx)=q, so x=-ln(q)/λ.
This is not NORM.INV. Exponential is asymmetric and uses ln, not z-scores.
SAY ALOUD
Inverse exponential means solve the exponential equation for time. Cumulative alpha gives x equals minus ln of one minus 
alpha over lambda.
Worked example - 80th percentile when λ=2 per week
1. Set P(X<x)=0.80.
2. 1-e^(-2x)=0.80, so e^(-2x)=0.20.
3. Take ln: -2x=ln(0.20).
4. x=-ln(0.20)/2≈0.8047 weeks.
Result: P80≈0.8047 weeks.
Verify: An 80th percentile is a positive waiting time. Substituting it back should give cumulative probability near 0.80.
KNOWLEDGE CHECK - answer before looking at the key
For cumulative probability α, which boundary formula is correct?
A. −ln(1−α)/λ
B. μ+zσ
C. ln(α)λ

7.1 - Population → Sample → Statistic → Sampling Distribution
OBJECTIVE
Build the core mental model: a sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a statistic over all possible random 
samples of a fixed size, not the raw observations in one sample.
Recognise it
The question asks what a sampling distribution is.
The question contrasts a population, one sample, one statistic and repeated possible statistics.
The wording asks why two random samples can produce different means or proportions.
Explanation
A population is the full collection of units. A sample is one subset drawn from it. A parameter such as μ, σ or π belongs to the 
population; a statistic such as Xk or p is calculated from a sample.
Different random samples usually contain different observations, so the statistic changes from sample to sample. The sampling 
distribution describes that variability mathematically.
The Chapter 7 objective is not merely to calculate one sample statistic; it is to reason about the distribution of the statistic 
before the sample is observed.
FORMULA / DECISION OBJECT
Sampling distribution = distribution of all possible values of a sample statistic for a fixed n. Random-object question: X, Xk, p, 
or Xk₁-Xk₂?
Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
1. Excel can calculate a realised sample statistic, but it does not decide what the random object is for you.
2. Before typing a function, name the statistic whose repeated values form the sampling distribution.
SAY ALOUD
One sample gives me one statistic. A sampling distribution is the distribution of that statistic across all possible random 
samples of the same size.
Worked example - Chapter 7 GPA thought experiment
1. Imagine repeatedly selecting 50 students from the same college.
2. Each sample produces a possibly different sample mean GPA.
3. Collect the possible sample means rather than the 50 raw GPAs from one sample.
4. The distribution of those possible means is the sampling distribution of Xk.
Result: The random object is Xk, and the sampling distribution is the probability model for its possible values.
Verify: Do not call one realised set of 50 GPAs a sampling distribution.
COMMON TRAP
Do not confuse population distribution, one sample, one realised statistic and the sampling distribution of that statistic.

7.2 - Exact Sampling Distributions & Unbiasedness
OBJECTIVE
Construct an exact sampling distribution for a tiny population and use it to see why Xk is an unbiased estimator of μ.
Recognise it
A tiny finite population is listed explicitly.
The task says "all possible samples" or asks you to construct the sampling distribution exactly.
The question asks why the sample mean is unbiased.
Explanation
The source builds a toy population 18,20,22,24 and lists every ordered sample of size 2 with replacement. Each possible sample 
has its own Xk.
An exact sampling distribution combines repeated statistic values with their probabilities. Its mean is the expected value of the 
statistic.
When E(Xk)=μ, Xk is an unbiased estimator of μ. Unbiased does not mean every sample mean equals μ.
FORMULA / DECISION OBJECT
μ_Xk = Σ Xk·P(Xk). Unbiasedness: E(Xk)=μ.
Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
1. For a small enumeration, Excel can list sample combinations and use AVERAGE for each sample, but the key idea is the 
complete set of possible statistic values.
2. For Tutorial 7 Q4, sampling is without replacement, so use combinations rather than duplicating ordered draws as if 
replacement were allowed.
SAY ALOUD
Unbiased means the sampling distribution is centred at the true parameter. One sample mean can miss μ; the expected 
sample mean still equals μ.
Worked example - Source toy population 18,20,22,24
1. Population mean μ=(18+20+22+24)/4=21.
2. With replacement and n=2 there are 4²=16 ordered samples.
3. Their sample means range from 18 to 24 and are no longer uniformly distributed.
4. The mean of the 16 sample means is 21.
Result: μ_Xk=21=μ, so Xk is unbiased for μ.
Verify: The sampling distribution can have a different shape and smaller spread than the population while keeping the same 
centre.
COMMON TRAP
Do not interpret unbiasedness as "Xk equals μ in every sample." It is a statement about the centre across possible samples.

7.3 - Standard Error & Why Larger n Narrows the Distribution
OBJECTIVE
Treat standard error as the standard deviation of a statistic across possible samples and explain exactly how n changes it.
Recognise it
The question compares variability of sample means from different n.
The question asks for the standard error of Xk.
The question asks why increasing sample size makes estimates more stable.
Explanation
Different samples produce different Xk values. The standard error measures the spread of those possible means.
For the ordinary sample-mean model (sampling with replacement, or without replacement from an effectively infinite 
population), σ_Xk=σ/√n. The square root matters: quadrupling n halves the standard error. If sampling is without replacement 
from a finite population, the FPC in Lesson 7.12 can reduce this spread further.
Increasing n does not shift the centre μ_Xk=μ. It narrows the distribution around the same target.
FORMULA / DECISION OBJECT
SE(Xk)=σ/√n. If n is multiplied by k, SE is divided by √k.
Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
1. In Excel use =sigma/SQRT(n) when population σ is known.
2. Do not use =sigma/n. SQRT(n) is the entire reason the gain from sample size has diminishing returns.
SAY ALOUD
Standard deviation describes individuals; standard error describes the statistic across samples. Larger n shrinks SE by the 
square-root rule, but the centre stays μ.
Worked example - Tutorial 7 Q4 comparison
1. For the population 1,3,6,7,9,10, all without-replacement n=2 sample means have mean 6.
2. Their exact sampling-distribution SD is 2.0000.
3. For all without-replacement n=3 sample means, the centre is still 6.
4. Their exact sampling-distribution SD is about 1.4142.
Result: The n=3 sampling distribution has less variability while retaining centre 6.
Verify: This exact Tutorial 7 Q4 enumeration is without replacement from N=6, so its SDs (2.0000 and 1.4142) already reflect 
finite-population reduction; they should not be mistaken for the ordinary σ/√n values. The key comparison is still that n=3 varies  
less than n=2.
COMMON TRAP
Do not divide by n; divide by √n. And do not say larger n changes the population standard deviation σ. Also do not use the 
uncorrected σ/√n formula to reproduce Tutorial 7 Q4's finite without-replacement enumeration; that case belongs to the FPC 
bridge.

8.1 - Point Estimates, Interval Estimates & Good Estimators
OBJECTIVE
Distinguish a population parameter from its sample estimator, distinguish point from interval estimation, and explain 
unbiasedness, consistency and relative efficiency in the terminology used by the Chapter 8 slides.
Recognise it
The question asks what one sample statistic estimates.
The question contrasts a single estimate with a range.
The question asks which estimator is unbiased, consistent or relatively more efficient.
Explanation
A population parameter is an unknown population characteristic such as the mean μ or proportion π. A sample statistic such as 
Xk or p is used to estimate it.
A point estimate is one number. A confidence interval adds information about precision by giving lower and upper confidence 
limits around a point estimate.
An unbiased estimator has expected value equal to the population parameter. A consistent estimator gets closer to the 
parameter as sample size increases. Among two unbiased estimators, the one with smaller variance is relatively more efficient.
FORMULA / DECISION OBJECT
Population mean μ ← estimated by Xk; population proportion π ← estimated by p. Point estimate = one statistic; interval 
estimate = point estimate plus quantified uncertainty.
Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
1. No special Excel function is needed for these definitions. In workbook questions, identify the statistic you are calculating 
before building any formula.
2. If raw values are supplied and the point estimate is a mean, AVERAGE(range) produces Xk; if the point estimate is a 
proportion, compute successes / n.
SAY ALOUD
The parameter belongs to the population; the estimator comes from the sample. A point estimate is one number. A 
confidence interval adds uncertainty. Unbiased means centred correctly, consistent means improving with larger n, and 
relative efficiency means smaller variance among unbiased estimators.
Worked example - Source estimation-process diagram
1. The population mean μ is unknown.
2. A random sample is drawn from the population.
3. The sample mean Xk=50 is the point estimate of μ.
4. An interval such as 40 to 60 adds a quantified range around the estimate.
Result: Xk is the point estimator; the confidence interval is the interval estimate for μ.
Verify: Do not describe a confidence interval as an interval for individual observations.
COMMON TRAP
Do not swap the population parameter with the sample statistic, and do not treat a confidence interval for a parameter as a 
range for individual observations.

8.2 - Confidence Level, α, Margin of Error & Correct Interpretation
OBJECTIVE
Understand what a confidence interval says, connect confidence level to α and α/2, identify margin of error, and avoid the 
fixed-interval probability mistake.
Recognise it
The question asks what "95% confidence" means.
The question asks for α or α/2 from a confidence level.
The question asks what widens or narrows an interval, or what the margin of error represents.
Explanation
The Chapter 8 source gives the general structure: point estimate ± (critical value)(standard error). The amount added and 
subtracted is the margin of error.
For a 95% confidence level, 1-α=0.95, so α=0.05 and α/2=0.025 in each tail for a two-sided interval.
The long-run interpretation is procedural: 95% of intervals constructed in this way would contain the unknown parameter. Once 
one particular interval has been calculated, the fixed parameter either lies in it or it does not.
FORMULA / DECISION OBJECT
Confidence interval = Point estimate ± Margin of error, where Margin of error = Critical value × Standard error.
Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper

the interval is two-sided.
2. For a 95% two-sided normal interval, the source uses zα/2=1.96.
SAY ALOUD
Confidence describes the long-run interval procedure, not a probability that the fixed parameter moves around. For 95%, 
alpha is 0.05 and each tail gets 0.025.
Worked example - Cereal-fill confidence interpretation
1. The source considers repeated samples of the same size.

3. Most intervals produced by the 95% procedure contain μ; some do not.

Result: Confidence is a property of the repeated procedure used to construct the interval.
Verify: Never reinterpret a confidence interval as the range containing 95% of individual data values.
COMMON TRAP
Do not say there is a 95% probability that the fixed parameter lies in the one interval already computed, and do not say 95% 
of individuals lie inside the CI.

8.9 - Finite Population Correction for Confidence Intervals
OBJECTIVE
Recognise when sampling without replacement from finite N requires the course FPC rule, understand why the correction 
reduces standard error, and apply it to mean/proportion intervals.
Recognise it
Sampling is without replacement from a finite population of size N.
The sampling fraction n/N must be checked.
The source uses the FPC when n/N>0.05; below 5% its practical effect is little or none.
Explanation
The finite-population source says use the correction factor when more than 5% of the population is sampled without 
replacement.
The factor √[(N-n)/(N-1)] multiplies the usual standard error. Because it is at most 1, it reduces the standard error and therefore 
narrows the confidence interval.
The same correction multiplies the mean standard error s/√n and the proportion standard error √[p(1-p)/n].
FORMULA / DECISION OBJECT
FPC = √[(N-n)/(N-1)]. Mean CI: Xk ± t(s/√n)FPC. Proportion CI: p ± z√[p(1-p)/n]FPC.
Excel route - rehearse the syntax on paper
1. FPC: =SQRT((N-n)/(N-1)).
2. Multiply the ordinary SE by the FPC before multiplying by the critical value.
3. Do not apply the correction merely because N is given; first check sampling without replacement and n/N.
SAY ALOUD
Finite population means without replacement. I check n over N. If it is more than five percent, the FPC multiplies and reduces 
the usual standard error.
Worked example - Finite-population source Example 8.10: N=200, n=27, =45.2222, s=23.1472, 95%X̄
1. Sampling is without replacement and n/N=27/200=.135>0.05, so FPC matters.
2. df=26 and the source uses t=2.0555.
3. FPC=√[(200-27)/(200-1)].
4. Apply t×(s/√n)×FPC as the margin.
5. The source obtains limits 36.6846 and 53.7598.
Result: 95% CI: (36.6846, 53.7598).
Verify: Because FPC<1, the interval is narrower than the corresponding no-FPC interval.
COMMON TRAP
Do not apply FPC merely because N appears in the question. The source requires sampling without replacement and uses 
n/N>0.05 as the practical trigger. Multiply the standard error by the FPC; do not add it.

9.2 - Decision Risk: α, β & Power
CH9 SCOPE
IN SCOPE: official Chapter 9 Slides 1-51. Later Chapter 9 sections are intentionally not printed in this exam-study pack.
OBJECTIVE
Understand the two possible testing errors and predict how α, β, power, effect size, variance and n move together.
Explanation
Type I error means rejecting H0 when H0 is true. Its probability is α, the significance level.
Type II error means not rejecting H0 when H0 is false. Its probability is β. Power is 1-β: the probability of rejecting a false H0.
The confidence coefficient is 1-α. Type I and Type II errors cannot occur in the same test outcome because H0 cannot 
simultaneously be true and false.
For fixed n, reducing α makes rejection harder and β increases. Power rises when the true parameter is farther from the null 
value or n increases; larger variance makes detection harder and increases β.
SAY ALOUD
Alpha is the chance I reject a true H0. Beta is the chance I fail to reject a false H0. Power is one minus beta. With fixed n, 
lowering alpha raises beta.
Worked example - Why distance from μ0 changes power
1. Suppose H0: μ=100.
2. A true μ=90 is 10 units from the null.
3. A true μ=75 is 25 units from the null.
4. The farther alternative creates more separation between the sampling distributions.
5. Therefore β is smaller and power larger at μ=75 than at μ=90.
Result: Closer alternatives are harder to detect; farther alternatives have higher power.
Verify: This is exactly the logic behind Tutorial 9 Q3.

The direction is determined by H1, not by the sign of the sample result.

9.4 - Mean Test - σ Known: Z Critical-Value
CH9 SCOPE
IN SCOPE: official Chapter 9 Slides 1-51. Later Chapter 9 sections are intentionally not printed in this exam-study pack.
OBJECTIVE
Recognise a known-σ one-sample mean test, compute Z, and make the critical-value decision.
Explanation
When the question tests a population mean μ and the population standard deviation σ is known, the Chapter 9 route is a Z test.
Under H0, the sample mean is compared with μ0 using its standard error σ/√n. The test statistic is Z=(Xk-μ0)/(σ/√n).
The critical value(s) come from α and the tail already chosen from H1. If Zcalc lands in the rejection region, reject H0; otherwise 
do not reject H0.
Finish with a sentence about the population claim - not just "reject".
SAY ALOUD
Mean plus known population sigma means Z. I use x-bar minus mu-zero over sigma divided by square root n, then compare 
with the rejection region set by H1 and alpha.
Worked example - Tutorial 9 paint cans
1. H0: μ=3.75; H1: μ≠3.75; α=.01.
2. σ=.08, n=50, Xk=3.73.
3. SE=.08/√50≈.011314.
4. Z=(3.73-3.75)/.011314≈-1.768.
5. Two-tail α=.01 gives critical values about ±2.576.
6. -1.768 is not in either rejection tail.
Result: Do not reject H0 at the 1% significance level.
Verify: The test statistic is not extreme enough for the strict .01 two-tail rejection region.

OBJECTIVE
Understand what the p-value means, calculate it for the correct tail, compare it with α, and reconstruct the deck's Excel 
route.
Explanation
The deck defines the p-value as the probability, assuming H0 is true, of obtaining a test statistic equal to or more extreme than 
the observed one.
Decision rule: if p<α, reject H0; if p≥α, do not reject H0.
Two-tail Z p-values use both tails. The source Excel formula is (1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(Zcalc),TRUE))*2. A directed one-tail p-value 
uses only the tail specified by H1.
A p-value is not the probability that H0 is true. It is calculated under the assumption that H0 is true.
SAY ALOUD
The p-value is an extremeness probability under H0. If p is smaller than alpha I reject H0; otherwise I do not reject H0.
Worked example - Tutorial 9 Q7
1. Two-tail test with Z=2.00.
2. One upper tail beyond 2.00 is about .02275.
3. Two-tail p≈2(.02275)=.0455.
4. Compare .0455 with α=.05.
5. .0455<.05, so reject H0.
Result: p≈.0455; reject H0 at α=.05.
Verify: Two-tail means count equally extreme results in both directions.
